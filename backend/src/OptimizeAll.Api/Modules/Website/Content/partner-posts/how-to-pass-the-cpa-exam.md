The Uniform CPA Exam is less a single test than a multi-month project: several sections, each sat separately, each demanding weeks of focused study, all to be passed within a limited credit window. Most candidates who fall short do not lack intelligence; they lack a plan that survives a full-time job, a busy season and the occasional failed section.

This guide sets out a realistic plan: how the exam is structured at a high level, how to order sections, how to structure a study week, how to practise simulations, and how to build a review system that holds up.

> Exam rules, section content, scoring, fees, testing windows and credit-window length are set by the AICPA, NASBA and the state boards, and they change. Check the official bodies' current requirements before you book anything.

## How the exam is structured (at a high level)

Under the current CPA licensure model, candidates take three **core** sections and one **discipline** section of their choice:

- **Core:** Auditing and Attestation (AUD), Financial Accounting and Reporting (FAR), and Taxation and Regulation (REG).
- **Discipline (choose one):** Business Analysis and Reporting (BAR), Information Systems and Controls (ISC), or Tax Compliance and Planning (TCP).

Sections mix multiple-choice questions with **task-based simulations** — case-style problems using exhibits, documents and spreadsheets. Simulations reward applied skill, which is why passive reading is not enough.

Once you pass your first section, a **credit window** starts: you must pass the remaining sections within a set period set by your jurisdiction. Plan the whole sequence before sitting the first section.

## Step 1: choose your section order deliberately

There is no universally correct order, but these principles help:

- **Start with the heaviest section while motivation is highest.** Many candidates start with FAR because of its breadth.
- **Pair sections with overlapping content.** Your discipline choice should build on a core section: BAR builds on FAR, TCP on REG, ISC on AUD's controls content.
- **Match sections to your calendar.** If you work in audit, avoid sitting AUD at the peak of busy season; if you work in tax, the same logic applies to REG.
- **Leave buffer for a retake.** Build your plan so that one failed section does not threaten your credit window.

## Step 2: estimate hours honestly

Candidates' study time varies widely with background. Rather than rely on generic figures, estimate your own:

1. List the major topic areas in the official blueprint for the section.
2. Rate each: **new**, **rusty** or **strong**.
3. Allocate time: new topics need roughly three times the hours of strong ones; rusty topics about twice.
4. Add 20–25% for final review and practice exams.

Then divide by your realistic weekly availability — not your ideal one. If you can reliably study 15 hours a week, plan on 15, not 25.

## Step 3: structure the week

A plan that works for many working candidates, at around 15 hours a week:

| Day | Session | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | 90 min | New content: learn one topic, make brief notes |
| Tue | 90 min | Multiple-choice practice on Monday's topic plus mixed review |
| Wed | 90 min | New content |
| Thu | 90 min | Multiple-choice practice plus error-log review |
| Fri | 60 min | Light review or rest |
| Sat | 3–4 hrs | Simulations, then a mixed practice set |
| Sun | 2–3 hrs | Catch-up, weekly review and next week's plan |

Three rules keep it working:

- **Practice follows learning within 24 hours.** Do not "finish the chapter" and practise next week.
- **Every session ends with a written action.** One line on what to revisit.
- **Protect the weekend simulation session.** Simulations are where marks are won and lost.

## Step 4: practise simulations like a professional

Simulations mimic real tasks: reconciling a schedule, completing a journal entry, reviewing documents, researching guidance. A method that helps:

1. **Scan the requirement before the exhibits.** Know what you are solving for.
2. **Map exhibits to requirements.** Note which document answers which part.
3. **Work in the order that earns marks fastest.** Many simulations have independent parts; do not get stuck on the first.
4. **Show structure in your working.** A clean worksheet reduces slips.
5. **Review against the solution line by line**, noting whether each error was conceptual, procedural or a misread.

For example, a revenue recognition simulation might present a contract with several performance obligations. Apply the five-step model under ASC 606 (identify the contract, identify performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it, recognise revenue when obligations are satisfied) explicitly in your working, rather than jumping to an answer. The structure itself prevents most errors.

## Step 5: build a review system that works

Rereading notes feels productive but does little for recall. What works is **retrieval** — pulling information from memory — spaced over time. A simple system:

- **Error log:** every question you get wrong goes in, with the reason and the fix.
- **Flashcards for rules and thresholds:** reviewed on an expanding schedule.
- **Mixed practice sets:** questions drawn from all topics studied so far, not just this week's.
- **Weekly "teach-back":** explain one weak topic aloud in five minutes without notes.

The science behind this is in [Spaced repetition and active recall for professional exams](/blog/spaced-repetition-and-active-recall-for-exams).

## Step 6: the final two weeks before a section

- **Week −2:** take a full-length practice exam under timed conditions. Analyse by topic and question type.
- **Days −13 to −4:** target your three weakest topics and your most frequent error type.
- **Days −3 to −1:** light review of your error log and flashcards; no new topics.
- **Exam day:** manage time by testlet, and do not let one hard simulation consume time owed to others.

## When a section does not go to plan

A failed section is common and recoverable. Use your score report to identify weaker content areas, adjust the plan, and rebook quickly while the material is fresh — within the constraints of your credit window.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which CPA section should I take first?

Many candidates start with FAR because it is broad and underpins other sections, but the best order depends on your background and work calendar. Pair your discipline section with the core section it builds on.

### How many hours a day should I study for the CPA exam?

Consistency matters more than daily volume. Many working candidates manage 1.5 to 2 hours on weekdays and longer sessions at weekends. Estimate total hours from the blueprint and your own strengths, then plan backwards from your exam date.

### How do I get better at task-based simulations?

Practise them weekly from early in your preparation, not only in the final weeks. Read the requirement first, map exhibits to requirements, work in the order that earns marks fastest, and review every error by type.

### What happens if my credit window runs out?

Credit rules are set by your jurisdiction. Check your state board's current rules and plan your section sequence with a buffer for at least one retake.
