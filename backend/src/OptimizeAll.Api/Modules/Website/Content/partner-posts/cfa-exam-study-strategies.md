The Chartered Financial Analyst (CFA) Program, run by CFA Institute, is known for its breadth and its demands on time. Three levels, a large curriculum, and question formats that shift from standalone multiple choice towards case-based item sets and written responses. Candidates rarely fail because a single topic is impossible; they fail because the volume outruns their plan, or because they learn the material but never practise it in the format they will face.

This guide focuses on strategies that hold up under pressure: planning backwards, item-set technique with a worked vignette, ethics practice and a review loop that builds speed.

> Curriculum content, topic weights, formats, exam windows, fees and recommended study hours are set by CFA Institute and change. Check CFA Institute's current requirements and the official curriculum for your level.

## How the levels differ (at a high level)

- **Level I** emphasises knowledge and comprehension of tools and concepts, mainly through standalone multiple-choice questions.
- **Level II** emphasises application and analysis through **item sets** — a case vignette followed by several questions.
- **Level III** emphasises synthesis and portfolio management, with item sets and **constructed-response** (written) questions.

Ethical and professional standards run through all three levels. CFA Institute has historically suggested that candidates plan for several hundred hours of study per level; use its current guidance as a starting point and adjust for your background.

## Strategy 1: plan backwards from exam day

1. **Fix the exam date** and mark the final four weeks as review-only.
2. **Divide the remaining weeks** among topics in proportion to their official weights and your familiarity.
3. **Schedule practice from week one.** Every topic block ends with questions in the exam's format.
4. **Build in two buffer weeks** for illness, work peaks or topics that take longer.

A candidate with 20 weeks and roughly 15 hours a week has about 300 hours: perhaps 200 for first-pass learning with practice, 20 for buffers and 80 for final review and mock exams.

## Strategy 2: learn in the format you will be tested in

At Level I, practise standalone questions from the start. At Levels II and III, move to item sets quickly — a candidate who has only practised single questions will be slow reading vignettes on exam day.

### Item-set technique: SKIM-TARGET-VERIFY

1. **SKIM the questions first** (not the answers), so you know what to look for in the vignette.
2. **TARGET the vignette:** read it once, marking data and statements the questions will need.
3. **VERIFY each answer** against the specific sentence or number in the vignette, not your memory of it.

### Worked mini-vignette

*An analyst reviews a bond with a face value of 1,000, a 5% annual coupon and three years to maturity. The yield to maturity is 6%. The analyst's colleague says: "Because the yield exceeds the coupon, the bond trades at a premium."*

**Question 1:** What is the bond's price, to the nearest unit?

```
Price = 50/1.06 + 50/1.06^2 + 1,050/1.06^3
      = 47.17 + 44.50 + 881.60
      = 973.27  (approximately 973)
```

**Question 2:** Is the colleague's statement correct?

No. When the yield to maturity exceeds the coupon rate, the bond trades at a **discount** to face value — which is consistent with the price of about 973.

The skills tested are typical of item sets: one calculation, one conceptual check, and a statement in the vignette designed to mislead a candidate who reads quickly. Verifying against the vignette catches it.

## Strategy 3: treat ethics as a skill, not a reading assignment

Ethics questions can decide close results. Strategies:

- **Learn each standard with a concrete violation example and a compliant alternative.**
- **Practise "which standard is at stake?"** before "was it violated?".
- **Watch for partial compliance.** An action can satisfy one standard and breach another.
- **Revisit ethics weekly** rather than cramming it at the end.

## Strategy 4: a review loop that builds speed

Speed on exam day comes from recall fluency, not from rushing. A weekly loop:

1. **Mixed question set** from all topics studied so far.
2. **Error log update** — topic, error type (concept, calculation, misread, formula recall), fix.
3. **Spaced flashcard review** for formulas and definitions.
4. **One timed item set or constructed response** at the target pace.

See [Spaced repetition and active recall for professional exams](/blog/spaced-repetition-and-active-recall-for-exams) for the evidence behind this loop.

## Strategy 5: mock exams and the final four weeks

- **Take at least two full mock exams** under timed conditions in the final month.
- **Analyse by topic and error type**, not just by score.
- **Rework every missed question** until you can explain the right answer without notes.
- **Taper in the final days:** light review, sleep and logistics.

## Level III constructed responses

Written answers reward precision and brevity:

- Answer the **command word** (*calculate*, *justify*, *determine*) exactly.
- Use **bullets** and show calculations with labels.
- **Justify with the case facts** — generic textbook statements earn little.
- Stop when the question is answered; extra material rarely adds marks.

## Five mistakes that cost candidates marks

1. **Front-loading reading, back-loading practice.** Candidates who "finish the curriculum" before starting questions discover their weak spots too late. Practise within 24 hours of learning each reading.
2. **Treating formulas as the goal.** Formulas matter, but many questions test interpretation: what a ratio implies, why an assumption fails, which method suits a situation. For every formula, write one sentence on what it tells you.
3. **Ignoring calculator fluency.** Time lost to keystroke errors adds up across a long exam. Practise the calculator functions you will actually use — time value of money, cash-flow worksheets, statistics — until they are automatic.
4. **Studying topics in isolation.** Financial statement analysis, equity valuation and fixed income connect. Interleave topics in your practice sets so you learn to recognise which tool a question needs.
5. **Letting one topic sink the plan.** If a reading takes twice as long as planned, time-box it, log the gaps and move on; come back during review rather than letting the whole schedule slide.

## A note on weekly rhythm

A sustainable week for many working candidates looks like two or three weekday sessions of 90 minutes (one new reading plus questions) and one longer weekend session for mixed practice and review. Protect the weekend review: it is where spaced retrieval and error-log work happen, and it keeps earlier topics from fading while you push forward.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many hours should I study for a CFA level?

CFA Institute publishes guidance on study time; historically it has suggested several hundred hours per level. Adjust for your background and check the current guidance.

### When should I start practising item sets?

As early as possible for Levels II and III — ideally within the first few weeks. Reading vignettes efficiently is a skill that takes time to build.

### How should I study ethics?

Learn each standard with examples of violations and compliant alternatives, practise identifying which standard is at stake, and review ethics weekly throughout your preparation.

### What is the best way to use mock exams?

Take them under realistic timing, then spend more time analysing than you spent sitting them. Categorise every error and rework every missed question.
