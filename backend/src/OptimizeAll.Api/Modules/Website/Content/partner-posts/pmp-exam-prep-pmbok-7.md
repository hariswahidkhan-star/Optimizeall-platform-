The Project Management Professional (PMP) exam from the Project Management Institute (PMI) is mostly situational: you read a scenario and choose what a project manager should do. Candidates who memorise inputs, tools and outputs often struggle, because the exam rewards a *mindset* — how a professional project manager thinks across predictive, agile and hybrid environments.

The principles in the *PMBOK Guide – Seventh Edition* are an excellent way to build that mindset. This guide shows how to use them to structure study, answer situational questions and connect the principles to the exam content outline.

> The PMP exam is based on PMI's Examination Content Outline (ECO), not on any single book, and PMI updates the ECO, eligibility rules, fees and format over time — it also publishes new editions of its standards. Check PMI's current requirements and the current ECO before you plan.

## The 12 principles of PMBOK 7

PMBOK 7 describes project management through principles rather than processes. In short form:

1. **Stewardship** — be diligent, respectful and caring.
2. **Team** — create a collaborative project team environment.
3. **Stakeholders** — engage stakeholders effectively.
4. **Value** — focus on value.
5. **Systems thinking** — recognise, evaluate and respond to system interactions.
6. **Leadership** — demonstrate leadership behaviours.
7. **Tailoring** — tailor the approach to the context.
8. **Quality** — build quality into processes and deliverables.
9. **Complexity** — navigate complexity.
10. **Risk** — optimise risk responses.
11. **Adaptability and resiliency** — embrace adaptability and resiliency.
12. **Change** — enable change to achieve the envisioned future state.

## The eight performance domains

PMBOK 7 also groups the work into eight performance domains: **Stakeholders, Team, Development Approach and Life Cycle, Planning, Project Work, Delivery, Measurement and Uncertainty.** These map naturally to the exam's broad focus on people, process and the business environment.

## Why principles help with situational questions

Situational questions often present four plausible actions. Principles give you a consistent way to rank them. For example:

- An option that bypasses the team to impose a decision conflicts with **Team** and **Leadership**.
- An option that escalates immediately without analysis conflicts with **Stewardship** and **Systems thinking**.
- An option that applies a rigid process where it clearly does not fit conflicts with **Tailoring**.
- An option that ignores an affected stakeholder conflicts with **Stakeholders**.

## The PRINCIPLE-FIRST answer method

1. **Identify the situation:** people issue, process issue or business-environment issue? Predictive, agile or hybrid context?
2. **Name the principle at stake** (one or two of the 12).
3. **Eliminate options that violate it.** Typically two options fall away.
4. **Choose the option that acts at the right level** — usually understanding and collaborating before escalating, and addressing root causes rather than symptoms.

### Worked example 1

*During sprint planning, two senior developers disagree strongly about a technical approach, and the discussion is becoming personal. What should the project manager do first?*

- A. Decide the approach to save time.
- B. Escalate to the functional managers.
- C. Facilitate a discussion focused on the problem and the trade-offs, helping the team reach a decision.
- D. Ask the product owner to choose.

**Principles:** Team, Leadership. **Answer: C.** The project manager acts as a servant leader and facilitator; imposing (A) or escalating (B) too early undermines the team, and the product owner (D) owns *what* to build, not *how*.

### Worked example 2

*A regulatory change will affect a deliverable in a predictive project nearing the end of design. What should the project manager do first?*

- A. Update the design immediately.
- B. Assess the impact on scope, schedule, cost and risk, then follow the change control process.
- C. Ignore it until the regulation takes effect.
- D. Ask the sponsor for extra budget.

**Principles:** Systems thinking, Change, Stewardship. **Answer: B.** Analyse before acting, and use the agreed change process. Acting without analysis (A), ignoring the issue (C) or jumping to a solution (D) all skip a step.

### Worked example 3

*An agile team's velocity has dropped for three consecutive iterations. The sponsor asks the project manager to "make the team work harder". What should the project manager do first?*

- A. Add overtime for the next two iterations.
- B. Report the decline to the sponsor with a recovery commitment.
- C. Work with the team, for example in a retrospective, to understand the causes of the decline.
- D. Reduce the size of the stories to raise velocity.

**Principles:** Team, Systems thinking, Leadership. **Answer: C.** Velocity is a planning tool, not a performance target. Understanding causes — impediments, unclear requirements, technical debt, team changes — comes before any action. A treats the symptom and risks burnout; B commits without understanding; D games the metric without improving delivery.

## Mindset traps to avoid

- **The hero trap:** choosing options where the project manager personally solves everything. The exam favours enabling the team.
- **The escalation trap:** escalating before trying to resolve issues at the appropriate level.
- **The process trap:** applying a formal process where a conversation would do — or skipping one where governance requires it.
- **The metric trap:** treating velocity, SPI or any single number as the goal rather than a signal.

## A 10-week study plan

At around 10–12 hours a week:

| Week | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1 | Read the current ECO; map its domains and tasks to the 12 principles and 8 performance domains |
| 2 | People: team building, conflict, leadership, stakeholder engagement |
| 3 | People continued: servant leadership, coaching, virtual teams |
| 4 | Process: planning, scope, schedule, cost, quality |
| 5 | Process: risk, procurement, change control, project closure |
| 6 | Agile and hybrid: roles, ceremonies, backlogs, value delivery, tailoring |
| 7 | Business environment: benefits, compliance, organisational change |
| 8 | Mixed practice sets and error-log review |
| 9 | Two full-length timed practice exams |
| 10 | Targeted review, light revision, rest |

Every week, do at least one mixed set of situational questions, and for each wrong answer record **which principle you misapplied**.

## Agile and hybrid: don't under-prepare

A large share of the PMP exam reflects agile and hybrid approaches. Understand roles (product owner, team, facilitator), artefacts (backlogs, increments), events (planning, reviews, retrospectives) and — most importantly — the values behind them. ## Using practice questions well

- **Quality over quantity.** Good explanations that justify *why* each wrong option is wrong teach more than hundreds of unexplained questions.
- **Mix contexts.** Practise predictive, agile and hybrid scenarios together, as they appear on the exam.
- **Review every question**, including those you got right by guessing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the PMP exam based on PMBOK 7?

The exam is based on PMI's Examination Content Outline. PMBOK 7 and other PMI publications are useful references; check the current ECO and PMI's list of references.

### Do I need to memorise the 12 principles?

Knowing them well is valuable because they give you a consistent way to evaluate situational answer options. Understand what each looks like in practice rather than memorising wording.

### How much of the PMP exam is agile?

A substantial portion of the exam reflects agile and hybrid approaches. Check the current ECO for PMI's stated balance.

### What is the best way to answer situational questions?

Identify the context and the principle at stake, eliminate options that violate it, and choose the action that analyses and collaborates at the right level before escalating.
