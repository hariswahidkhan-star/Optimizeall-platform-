## Article

Ask people what they would like in a new career and you often hear the same list: analytical work, variety, a clear
link between effort and results, and skills that won't be automated away next year. Few of them have heard of project
controls — yet it offers all four, and it welcomes people from surprisingly different backgrounds.

## What project controls is

Every major project — a rail line, a hospital, a data centre, a drug-manufacturing plant, an enterprise software
rollout — needs people who can answer three questions with evidence: where are we, where are we heading, and what will
it take to land it? Project controls professionals build the plans, measure progress and cost, forecast outcomes and
help leaders decide what to do.

Typical tasks include building schedules, tracking budgets and commitments, measuring progress, calculating earned
value, forecasting the final cost and finish date, managing change and risk, and presenting options to decision-makers.

## Why career changers do well

Project controls is unusually open to people from other fields because it combines skills that develop in many roles:

| If you've worked as... | You already bring... |
|---|---|
| An engineer or technician | Understanding of how work is sequenced and built |
| An accountant or finance analyst | Budgets, accruals, cost reporting, reconciliation |
| A quantity surveyor or estimator | Estimating, valuations, contracts |
| A military logistics or operations professional | Planning under constraints, reporting, discipline |
| A data analyst | Data cleaning, automation, dashboards |
| A project coordinator | Stakeholder management, documentation, follow-through |

What you add is the connecting method: logic-linked scheduling, earned value, forecasting and change control.

## A quick self-assessment

Score yourself from 0 (no experience) to 2 (confident) on each:

1. Breaking a large piece of work into smaller parts and sequencing them.
2. Building and maintaining a budget or cost report.
3. Measuring progress objectively rather than by feel.
4. Working with spreadsheets or data tools beyond the basics.
5. Explaining bad news clearly and calmly with evidence.
6. Reading contracts or specifications for obligations and dates.

A score of 8 or more suggests you are closer than you think. Below 5, you have a clear list of what to learn first.

## A 90-day break-in plan

**Days 1–30: learn the core methods.** Study critical-path scheduling and earned value. Build a small schedule for a
realistic project (a house extension, an office move) with 50–80 activities and proper logic. Calculate earned value
for three imaginary reporting periods.

**Days 31–60: build evidence.** Turn your practice into a short portfolio: the schedule, a one-page monthly report with
a forecast and a variance explanation, and a brief note on how you would govern AI use on project data. Employers are
far more persuaded by one clean example than by a list of software names.

**Days 61–90: connect and apply.** Talk to planners and cost engineers (professional associations and online
communities are good starting points). Look for junior planner, cost controller, project controls analyst or
coordinator roles. In applications, translate your background: "reconciled 3,000 monthly transactions" becomes evidence
of cost-control discipline.

## Translating your experience on a CV

Hiring managers in project controls scan for evidence of three things: structured planning, cost discipline and clear
communication. Rewrite achievements from your current role in those terms:

- **Finance:** "Reconciled 3,000 monthly transactions across 40 cost centres; reduced month-end adjustments by
  introducing accrual checks" → evidence of cost control and data quality.
- **Engineering or site work:** "Sequenced installation of 120 units across four sites with a three-week look-ahead" →
  evidence of scheduling logic and field reality.
- **Military or operations:** "Planned logistics for a 200-person deployment with fixed dates and constrained
  transport" → evidence of planning under constraints and reporting discipline.
- **Data:** "Automated a weekly performance report from three source systems" → evidence of data skills controls teams
  badly need.

Add one line on outcomes wherever you can: what decision your work enabled, what problem it caught early.

## What the first year looks like

Most people starting in controls spend their first months learning the organisation's tools and procedures, updating
schedules or cost reports under supervision, and preparing inputs for monthly reports. The steepest learning comes from
the monthly cycle itself: seeing how progress, cost and risk come together, and how leaders react to the numbers. Ask to
sit in on reviews with project managers and sponsors; understanding what decision-makers need is what turns an analyst
into a trusted adviser.

A useful habit from day one: keep a personal log of every variance you help explain and what caused it. Within a year you
will have a practical library of how projects actually go off track — and that experience is what employers value most.

## How AI is changing the role — in your favour

AI is automating parts of project controls: classifying cost data, checking schedules for logic errors, drafting
variance narratives, generating forecast ranges from historical projects. That might sound like a threat. In practice,
it shifts value towards the skills career changers often bring — judgement, communication, data literacy and
governance.

Someone has to decide which tools can process which data, check AI outputs against explainable methods, and stand in
front of a steering committee to explain a forecast. That person needs to understand both the fundamentals and the
limits of the tools.

## Questions to ask in interviews

Interviews are also your chance to judge the role. Useful questions: How is progress measured here — by rules of credit
or estimates? How often are forecasts updated, and who reviews them? What does the controls team present to leadership
each month? How does the organisation govern the use of AI tools on project data? The answers tell you how mature the
function is and how much you will learn in it.

## Do you need a qualification?

Not to get your first role — many people start through internal moves or junior positions. But a structured credential
can help career changers in two ways: it fills gaps systematically, and it gives employers an independent signal when
your CV comes from another field.

When comparing options, look at eligibility, the scope of the body of knowledge, how the exam is assessed and how the
credential treats AI. Some are designed with career changers in mind; for example, [a project controls credential open
to people from any field](https://pciai.org) with three years' experience assesses planning, cost, earned value,
forecasting and governed AI through online scenario-based exams.

## The takeaway

If you like structure, numbers and people — and want work where your analysis visibly changes decisions — project
controls deserves a look. You probably already have half the skills. The other half can be learned in months, and the
first step is building one small schedule this week.
