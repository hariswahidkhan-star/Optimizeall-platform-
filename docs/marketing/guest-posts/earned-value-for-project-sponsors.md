## Article

Every month, project sponsors receive reports full of acronyms: PV, EV, AC, CPI, SPI, EAC. Many nod, glance at whether
the indicators are green, and move on. That is a missed opportunity. Earned value is one of the most powerful early
warning systems in project management — but only if the people who make decisions know what it is telling them.

This guide explains earned value without the jargon, walks through one example, and gives you five questions to ask at
your next steering committee.

## The idea in one sentence

Earned value answers: **for the money we have spent, how much of the planned work have we actually achieved?**

A project can be "on budget" in the sense that it has spent exactly what it planned — and still be in serious trouble
if it has achieved only half the work it planned for that money. Earned value exposes that.

## The three numbers

- **Planned value (PV):** how much work, measured in budget terms, *should* be done by now.
- **Earned value (EV):** how much work, measured in the same budget terms, *is* done.
- **Actual cost (AC):** what that done work *actually cost*.

Two ratios summarise them:

- **Cost performance index (CPI) = EV ÷ AC.** Below 1.0 means each unit of money spent is producing less work than planned.
- **Schedule performance index (SPI) = EV ÷ PV.** Below 1.0 means less work is done than planned by this date.

## One example

A programme to refurbish 40 railway stations has a budget of 20 million — 0.5 million per station. By month six, the
plan says 20 stations should be complete: **PV = 10 million**.

In reality, 16 stations are complete, and partial progress on others adds the equivalent of 2 more: **EV = 9 million**.
Finance reports **AC = 10 million** spent.

- CPI = 9 ÷ 10 = **0.90**
- SPI = 9 ÷ 10 = **0.90**

The headline "we've spent 10 million against a 10 million plan" sounds fine. Earned value says otherwise: the programme
is getting 90% of the planned work for its money and is 10% behind where it should be.

If that cost efficiency continues, the programme will cost about 20 ÷ 0.90 = **22.2 million** — an overrun of more than
2 million. At month six, there is still time to act. At month eighteen, there would not be.

## What earned value does not tell you

Earned value tells you *that* performance is off plan, not *why*. The station programme might be losing efficiency
because of access restrictions, design changes, supplier delays or productivity problems. Each needs a different
response. Good controls teams pair the numbers with root-cause analysis; good sponsors ask for it.

It also depends on honest inputs. If progress is claimed by gut feel rather than agreed rules, or if costs lag because
invoices haven't arrived, the indices can look healthier than reality.

## Reading the four combinations

CPI and SPI together tell four different stories, and each calls for a different conversation:

| CPI | SPI | What it usually means | Conversation to have |
|---|---|---|---|
| At or above 1.0 | At or above 1.0 | Efficient and on pace | Is the plan realistic, or was it padded? Are risks ahead? |
| At or above 1.0 | Below 1.0 | Efficient but slow — often under-resourced | Can we add capacity without losing efficiency? What is on the critical path? |
| Below 1.0 | At or above 1.0 | On pace but overspending — often buying speed | Is the extra spend deliberate (for example, overtime to protect a milestone) and is it worth it? |
| Below 1.0 | Below 1.0 | Slow and expensive | What is the root cause? Is the baseline still valid? Do we need a formal re-forecast? |

Look at trends as well as the current month. A CPI that has slipped from 0.98 to 0.90 over three months is more
worrying than a steady 0.93, because it suggests the underlying problem is growing.

## Why late-project SPI can mislead

One technical caveat worth knowing: as a project approaches completion, SPI drifts back towards 1.0 even if the project
is late, because eventually all planned work is earned. In the final third of a project, ask your controls team for the
critical-path forecast finish date alongside SPI — or for an "earned schedule" measure, which corrects the drift.

## Five questions to ask at your next steering committee

1. **"How is progress measured?"** You want to hear about agreed rules of credit (milestones, units complete, weighted
   steps), not estimates of percentage complete.
2. **"Do actual costs include accruals?"** If work done but not yet invoiced is excluded, CPI will be flattering.
3. **"What is the forecast at completion, and what range around it?"** A single number hides uncertainty; a range with
   named drivers helps you decide.
4. **"What is causing the variance, and what are the options?"** Reports should end with options and a recommendation,
   not just charts.
5. **"What would it take to finish on budget?"** Ask for the to-complete performance index — the efficiency needed on
   the remaining work. If it is far better than anything achieved so far, the budget is no longer realistic, and it is
   better to know now.

## What a good earned-value report looks like

If you could ask for only one page each month, ask for this: the current CPI and SPI with their three-month trend; the
forecast cost and finish date as a range, with the two or three drivers behind it; the top variances with root causes;
the options for recovery with their cost and schedule effect; and the controls team's recommendation. A report built this
way takes minutes to read and turns the steering committee from an audience into a decision-making body.

## A note on AI

AI tools increasingly help controls teams prepare earned-value data, draft variance narratives and produce forecast
ranges from historical projects. That can be valuable, but it adds a sixth question: **"Which parts of this report were
AI-assisted, and who checked them?"** A good answer names the tool, the data, the comparison with a conventional
calculation and the reviewer.

## Why this matters for sponsors

Sponsors are accountable for the investment. Earned value gives you the earliest reliable signal that a project is
drifting — typically long before the budget is exhausted or a milestone is missed. Understanding the three numbers and
asking five questions is a small investment for that kind of foresight.

For controls professionals, the flip side is to make the numbers speak plainly — which is why earned value, forecasting
and clear decision-ready reporting feature prominently in modern [project controls certification](https://pciai.org)
and training. When both sides of the table speak the same language, steering committees make better decisions, sooner.
