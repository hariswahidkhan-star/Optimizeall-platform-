## Article

The Next Generation NCLEX (NGN) changed what readiness for practice looks like on the licensure exam. Items built on
the NCSBN Clinical Judgment Measurement Model ask candidates to recognise cues, analyse them, prioritise hypotheses,
generate solutions, take action and evaluate outcomes — the thinking nurses do at the bedside, not only the knowledge
they recall.

Question banks help students practise formats. But clinical judgment is built through repeated, guided reasoning, and
that happens best in classrooms and simulation labs. Here are five low-preparation activities that map to the model and
work for both RN and PN programmes.

(Test plans and item formats are set by the NCSBN and updated periodically; align activities with the current test plan
for your programme.)

## 1. Cue hunt (recognise cues)

**What it is:** Give small groups a short nurse's note, a vital-signs table and a medication list for a fictional
client. Ask them to highlight every finding that requires follow-up — and, just as important, to justify why each
un-highlighted finding does not.

**Why it works:** NGN highlight items reward selective attention. Students who highlight everything "to be safe" are
practising the wrong habit. Justifying non-selections trains discrimination.

**Debrief question:** "Which cue would you have missed if you'd only read the first half of the note?"

## 2. Trend walk (analyse cues)

**What it is:** Present the same client's vital signs at four time points. Students plot them on a whiteboard and
describe what is changing, how fast and what it might mean.

**Worked example:** A post-operative client's values over eight hours show temperature rising from 37.2 °C to 38.4 °C,
heart rate from 88 to 112, respiratory rate from 18 to 24, blood pressure falling from 124/78 to 102/64, and new
confusion. No single value is dramatic in isolation; the combined trend is the signal. Students discuss what hypotheses
the trend supports and what additional data they would want.

**Why it works:** NGN trend items and unfolding case studies test exactly this: judgement based on direction and pace
of change rather than single values.

## 3. Hypothesis ladder (prioritise hypotheses)

**What it is:** From the trend walk, groups list every possible explanation, then rank them by likelihood and by
urgency — two separate columns. Where likelihood and urgency disagree, groups must decide which drives action and why.

**Why it works:** Prioritisation is where many students struggle. Separating "most likely" from "most dangerous" makes
the reasoning explicit and discussable.

## 4. Action sort (generate solutions and take action)

**What it is:** Give groups a set of cards with possible nursing actions — some appropriate, some premature, some
outside scope, some harmful. Groups sort them into "do now", "do later", "not appropriate" and "outside my scope",
then justify each placement.

**RN and PN variation:** Run the same sort from both perspectives. For RN students, include delegation and care-plan
revision cards. For PN students, emphasise recognising changes, reporting to the RN or provider, and implementing care
within the plan. The contrast strengthens understanding of scope, which NGN items reflect in the actions they expect.

**Why it works:** Multiple-response and matrix items reward choosing only what the evidence supports. Sorting forces
explicit judgement about each option.

## 5. Outcome check (evaluate outcomes)

**What it is:** After a simulated intervention, present new data. Students decide, for each finding, whether it shows
improvement, no change or deterioration, and what the nurse should do next.

**Why it works:** Evaluation is the most neglected step in student preparation — and NGN case studies commonly end with
it. Practising evaluation closes the loop and builds the habit of reassessment.

## Putting the five together: a 50-minute session plan

| Minutes | Activity | Clinical-judgment step |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Introduce the client and state the steps for today | — |
| 5–15 | Cue hunt on the admission note | Recognise cues |
| 15–25 | Trend walk on four sets of vital signs | Analyse cues |
| 25–32 | Hypothesis ladder in pairs | Prioritise hypotheses |
| 32–42 | Action sort with scope variation | Generate solutions / take action |
| 42–48 | Outcome check with new data | Evaluate outcomes |
| 48–50 | Each student writes one sentence: "Next time I will notice..." | Reflection |

The same client can carry across two or three sessions, with new data each time — mirroring an unfolding case study and
letting students see the consequences of earlier decisions.

## Assessing the activities

Formative assessment works best here. Short rubrics with three levels (not yet, developing, secure) for each step let
educators see where a cohort struggles — often prioritisation and evaluation — and adjust future sessions. Students can
self-assess with the same rubric, which reinforces the vocabulary of clinical judgment.

## Making it stick

- **Name the step.** Start each activity by stating which clinical-judgment step it targets. Over a term, students start
  to label questions themselves — a powerful exam habit.
- **Rotate content areas.** Use medical-surgical, maternal-newborn, paediatric and mental-health cases so reasoning
  transfers.
- **Use short cycles.** Twenty-minute activities once or twice a week beat an occasional three-hour workshop.
- **Connect to independent practice.** Ask students to follow each activity with a small set of NGN-style questions on
  the same step, and to log any errors by step (missed cue, misinterpretation, wrong priority, wrong action).

## Adapting for large cohorts and online teaching

The activities scale well. In large lecture groups, use polling tools for the cue hunt and outcome check, then ask two
or three students to justify their choices aloud. Online, breakout rooms work for the hypothesis ladder and action sort,
with a shared document for each group. For asynchronous courses, post the case in stages — a new data set every few
days — and ask students to submit a short justification at each stage before the next is released. The key ingredient in
every format is the same: students commit to a judgement and explain it before they see the answer.

## Supporting independent study

Between classes, students need realistic practice and feedback. Encourage tools that make students explain their
reasoning rather than simply revealing answers, and that separate guided practice from timed mock exams. Some
platforms now offer [NCLEX-RN and NCLEX-PN practice with an AI coach that guides reasoning](https://certuvo.com) through
questions rather than answers and switches itself off during mock exams, which reinforces the same habits you build in
class.

## Conclusion

The NGN rewards thinking that nurse educators have always valued. The difference now is that the exam measures it
explicitly, step by step. Short, repeated classroom activities that name each step — and give students practice
justifying what they *don't* select — build the judgement the exam looks for and, more importantly, the judgement
patients need.
