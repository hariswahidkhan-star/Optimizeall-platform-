## Article

IT audit sits at the intersection of technology, risk and governance. IT auditors assess whether an organisation's
systems are well governed, secure, reliable and compliant — and whether the controls that management relies on actually
work. As organisations automate more processes and adopt AI, the demand for people who can provide independent
assurance over technology keeps growing.

This roadmap explains the career ladder, the skills that matter, the mindset shift technical professionals need to
make, and where the Certified Information Systems Auditor (CISA) credential from ISACA fits.

## What IT auditors do

A typical year might include:

- Testing **IT general controls**: access management, change management, backups, operations.
- Reviewing **application controls** in finance, payroll or procurement systems.
- Assessing **cybersecurity** programmes against frameworks.
- Reviewing **system implementations** and major IT projects.
- Evaluating **business continuity and disaster recovery** arrangements.
- Supporting financial-statement audits with technology testing.
- Increasingly, providing assurance over **data governance and AI systems**.

## The career ladder

**IT audit associate or analyst (years 0–2).** Executes test procedures, documents evidence, drafts findings. Learns
frameworks, audit methodology and the organisation's systems.

**Senior IT auditor (years 2–5).** Plans and leads audit areas, supervises juniors, discusses findings with system
owners, and begins to shape risk-based audit plans.

**IT audit manager (years 5–8).** Owns a portfolio of audits, manages stakeholders up to senior management, and
contributes to the annual audit plan and audit-committee reporting.

**Director or head of IT audit / technology risk.** Sets strategy, builds the team, and advises the board on technology
risk.

People also move sideways into GRC, security assurance, internal controls and risk-consulting roles.

## The mindset shift for technical professionals

Technical candidates bring enormous value — they understand how systems really work and where they break. But the
auditor role differs in three ways:

1. **Risk first.** Before testing anything, an auditor asks what could go wrong and how much it would matter.
2. **Evidence over expertise.** Conclusions rest on evidence the auditor can document — observation, re-performance,
   independent data — not on personal knowledge of how a system "usually" works.
3. **Recommend, don't fix.** Auditors must remain independent. Designing or implementing the fix would compromise that
   independence; management owns remediation.

### A worked example

*During an audit, you find that developers can deploy code to production without independent review. What do you do
first?*

An engineer's instinct is to remove the access or recommend a new tool. The auditor's first step is to **understand the
risk and look for compensating controls** — for example, whether all production changes are independently reviewed after
deployment, or monitored and reconciled to approved tickets. Only then can you judge how significant the finding is and
recommend proportionate improvements. That sequence — assess, evaluate, then recommend — is the essence of the role.

## Skills that matter

- **Frameworks:** familiarity with IT governance and control frameworks (COBIT is widely used), security frameworks and
  relevant regulations.
- **Audit methodology:** risk assessment, evidence, sampling, documentation, reporting.
- **Technology breadth:** operating systems, databases, networks, cloud, identity management, software development.
- **Data analytics:** testing whole populations with audit software or scripting, not just samples.
- **Communication:** writing findings that are clear, fair and actionable; presenting to non-technical audiences.

## How AI is changing IT audit

AI brings new work to IT audit in two directions:

- **Auditing AI:** assessing governance over AI models and tools — approved uses, data protection, model validation,
  monitoring, accountability. Auditors need enough understanding to ask the right questions.
- **Auditing with AI:** using AI to analyse logs, classify transactions, draft working papers and identify anomalies —
  under the same rules of evidence and review that apply to any audit procedure.

Both reinforce the auditor's core value: independent, evidence-based judgement.

## Where the CISA fits

The CISA is one of the most widely recognised IT audit credentials. Its job practice covers the information-system
auditing process; governance and management of IT; information-systems acquisition, development and implementation;
operations and business resilience; and protection of information assets. ISACA sets experience requirements for
certification, with some substitutions available, and updates the exam outline periodically — check ISACA's current
requirements.

For career changers, preparing for the CISA is also an efficient way to learn the auditor mindset, because the exam
consistently rewards "think like an auditor" answers over "fix it like an engineer" answers.

Preparation tips:

- Study each domain from the auditor's perspective: risks, controls, evidence.
- Practise scenario questions early, and log errors by type — especially "role confusion".
- Use flashcards for terms that need precision, such as recovery time and recovery point objectives.
- Use practice material that explains *why* the auditor's answer is right; tools such as [CISA practice questions with
  an AI coach](https://certuvo.com) that asks guiding questions can help build the reasoning habit.

## A day in the life: testing a change-management control

To make the work concrete, consider a common IT general control: "All changes to the payroll system are approved
before deployment." An IT auditor might:

1. **Understand the process** by walking through it with the change manager and reading the procedure.
2. **Identify the population** — every production change in the period — from the deployment logs, not from the
   ticketing system alone, so changes that bypassed the process are included.
3. **Test** by matching every deployment to an approved ticket with approval dated before deployment, using data
   analytics on the full population where possible.
4. **Investigate exceptions** — emergency changes, missing approvals, approvals after the fact — and judge whether they
   indicate a control failure.
5. **Report** a clear finding with the risk, the root cause and a proportionate recommendation, and agree an action
   plan with management.

Every step reflects the auditor mindset: independent sources, complete populations, evidence and proportion.

## Ethics and independence in practice

IT auditors often know system owners well and are asked for advice. Advisory work can be valuable, but auditors must
avoid designing the controls they will later test, and must disclose relationships or previous roles that could affect
objectivity. Professional codes — including ISACA's — make these obligations explicit, and they are part of what gives
audit conclusions their credibility.

## Getting started this month

1. Read an IT general controls audit programme and map each test to the risk it addresses.
2. Volunteer to support an internal or external audit of a system you know.
3. Learn one audit-analytics technique — for example, reconciling a full export of change tickets to production changes.
4. Decide whether and when to pursue the CISA, and plan your study around work peaks.

IT audit rewards curiosity, integrity and clear thinking. If you already understand technology, the auditor mindset is
the missing piece — and it can be learned.
