Scene 1 — Building a list with consent

```text
Here's a question that separates healthy email programmes from struggling ones. How did the people on your list get there? If the answer is: they asked, clearly, for something specific, you've got an asset. If the answer is: we bought a list, we scraped some addresses, or we ran a giant giveaway, you've got a liability that will quietly damage your deliverability and your reputation. In this lecture you'll learn why consent is the foundation of email, how to design lead magnets and forms that people want to fill in, how double opt-in works, and how to measure whether your sign-ups are any good.
```

Scene 2 — Why email matters

```text
Why does email still matter so much? Because it's an owned channel. No algorithm decides whether your subscribers see your message, and your list stays yours even if a social platform changes its rules overnight. For creators, e-commerce brands, coaches and B2B firms, a healthy list is often one of the most reliable sources of revenue. But that only holds if people genuinely want your emails. Think of it like being invited into someone's home. If you were invited, you're a welcome guest. If you climbed through the window, you'll be shown the door, and mailbox providers are the neighbourhood watch.
```

Scene 3 — Consent first

```text
So build on consent. Tell people exactly what they'll get and how often, like weekly tips on freelance pricing, plus occasional offers. Use unticked opt-in boxes where consent is needed; pre-ticked boxes aren't valid consent under the GDPR and UK GDPR. Keep records of when, where and how each person signed up, including the wording they saw. Never buy, rent or scrape lists. Purchased lists contain spam traps and invalid addresses, break the law in many countries, and destroy trust. And consider double opt-in, a confirmation email before someone is added. It improves list quality and gives you proof of consent, at the cost of a few sign-ups.
```

Scene 4 — Lead magnets

```text
Now the lead magnet: something useful offered in exchange for an email address. The best ones are specific, quick to consume and closely related to what you sell. A freelance designer offers a brand checklist for new restaurants. A skincare brand offers a skin-type quiz with a personalised routine. A UK accountant offers a self-assessment deadline calendar. An online Arabic tutor offers a free trial lesson and printable worksheet. A B2B software company offers a template or calculator. Discount-led magnets grow lists fast but can attract bargain hunters. Value-led magnets tend to attract more engaged subscribers. Many brands use both.
```

Scene 5 — Collecting sign-ups

```text
Next, where and how to collect. Website forms on your homepage, blog footers and landing pages. Pop-ups, used carefully: after some engagement, easy to close, and not covering content on mobile. Checkout, with a separate, optional marketing opt-in. Social media, through a link-in-bio landing page or lead ads with clear consent wording. Events and shops, with a QR code to a sign-up page. And the form itself: a benefit headline, as few fields as possible, a consent statement with a privacy link, and a specific button. Send me the checklist beats submit every time. And only use social proof like join three thousand freelancers if it's true.
```

Scene 6 — Example 1: Lahore clothing store

```text
Let's do a simple worked example. A Lahore online clothing store adds three sign-up routes. A value-led quiz, find your Eid outfit style, with results emailed. A ten percent first-order code offered in an exit-intent pop-up on desktop and a small banner on mobile. And an unticked marketing checkbox at checkout. Every sign-up gets a double opt-in email. And the store records which source each subscriber came from. Thirty days later, it compares them. The quiz brings fewer sign-ups than the discount, but those subscribers click far more. That tells the store where to invest.
```

```text

Signed by a real person. Send it instantly, keep the button above the fold, and track the confirmation rate. ```text

Scene 1 — SMS marketing basics

```text
Think about the last text message you got from a business. Was it useful, like your parcel is out for delivery? Or was it one of those texts you delete before finishing the first line? SMS is powerful because it reaches almost any phone and gets read quickly. That's exactly why it's so easy to abuse, and why regulators and mobile carriers treat it strictly. In this lecture you'll learn when SMS earns its place, the consent and registration rules in the US, UK, UAE, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, how to write texts people welcome, why message length affects cost, and how to keep one suppression list across every channel.
```

Scene 2 — When SMS works

```text
So when does SMS earn its place? For time-sensitive, high-value messages people asked for. Delivery and appointment updates. One-time passcodes. Back-in-stock alerts for people who requested them. And occasional flash sales or early access for VIPs who opted in. Here's the key idea: SMS is the most intimate marketing channel after a phone call. It sits between messages from family and friends. So keep it short, rare and wanted. If a text wouldn't feel welcome coming from a friend who works at your company, don't send it.
```

Scene 3 — US rules

```text
Now the rules, starting with the US. The Telephone Consumer Protection Act generally requires prior express written consent for marketing texts sent with automated systems. You must honour opt-outs like STOP promptly, follow calling-hour limits, and check stricter state laws. There's also registration. Business texting from standard ten-digit numbers requires what's called A2P ten D L C registration of your brand and campaign. Toll-free numbers need verification, and short codes have their own approval. And industry guidelines expect clear opt-in, a frequency disclosure, and HELP and STOP keywords. Your SMS provider walks you through it, but you're responsible for the consent.
```

Scene 4 — UK/EU, UAE, KSA, Pakistan

```text
In the UK and EU, the PECR and ePrivacy rules generally require consent for marketing texts to individuals, with the UK soft opt-in for existing customers about your own similar products, and an opt-out in every message. Always identify the sender and offer a free, simple opt-out. In the UAE, the telecoms regulator, the TDRA, requires registered promotional sender IDs with an AD dash prefix, restricts promotional texts to daytime hours, commonly cited as seven a.m. to nine p.m., and requires opt-out instructions. In Saudi Arabia, sender names are registered through licensed providers, and the data protection law requires consent and an opt-out. And in Pakistan, the PTA regulates bulk SMS, so use licensed, registered providers.
```

Scene 5 — Welcome texts

```text
Now, writing texts people welcome. Identify yourself first, like Leaf and Loom colon, at the start. One message, one action, one link, ideally a short link on your own domain, because generic link shorteners can look like scams. Always include the opt-out: reply STOP to opt out. And respect time: local time zones, quiet hours, Friday prayers and Ramadan evenings. Here's a simple example from the lesson text. Leaf and Loom: VIP early access, new linen kurtas live now, twenty-four hours before everyone else, then a short link, then reply STOP to opt out. About a hundred and forty characters. Clear, useful and easy to leave.
```

Scene 6 — Length and cost

```text
Let's talk about length, because it affects cost. Standard SMS uses an alphabet called GSM seven, which allows a hundred and sixty characters per segment. But if your message includes characters outside it, including many emojis, and Arabic or Urdu script, it switches to Unicode, called UCS two, and each segment holds only seventy characters. So a hundred-and-fifty-character Arabic message might be billed as three segments instead of one. The lesson text has a quick Google Sheets formula to count characters and flag Unicode, though your provider's preview is the final word. Short messages aren't just better manners. They're cheaper.
```

Scene 7 — Example 1: Texas apparel (illustrative)

```text
Let's do a simple worked example, with illustrative results. A Texas apparel brand launches SMS. It registers its brand and campaign for A2P ten D L C through its provider. It adds an SMS opt-in step right after email sign-up, with the required disclosures: message frequency varies, message and data rates may apply, reply HELP for help and STOP to cancel. And it caps marketing texts at four a month. VIP launches by text sell out faster than email launches, and opt-out rates stay low. The discipline is the product: registered, consented, disclosed, and capped.
```

Scene 8 — Example 2: Dubai restaurants (illustrative)

```text
Now a realistic Gulf scenario. A Dubai restaurant group uses SMS for reservation confirmations, which are transactional, and a monthly offer to diners who opted in, sent from an AD dash sender ID, before the evening cut-off, with opt-out instructions. WhatsApp handles conversational bookings, like changing a table for four to six. And email carries the monthly newsletter with the menu and events. Each channel does the job it's best at. That's the real lesson of this module: SMS, WhatsApp and email aren't competitors. They're a team, as long as consent and suppression connect them.
```

Scene 9 — Suppression + mistakes

```text
Which brings us to suppression. Opt-outs must be honoured where they apply. Connect SMS, email and WhatsApp tools to a single consent and suppression record, like the one in the compliance lesson. A STOP by text stops SMS marketing. Decide, and document, whether it also affects other channels. And never re-add someone without new consent. Common mistakes: texting numbers collected for delivery as if they were marketing opt-ins, long emoji-heavy messages that split into costly segments, missing sender identification or opt-out, and sending at night or across time zones without checking.
```

Scene 10 — Measure success

```text
How do you measure success? Track your opt-in rate, your opt-out rate per send, which should stay low, and your delivery rate. Compare click-through and revenue per message sent with email and WhatsApp. And calculate cost per conversion including segment costs, because a channel that looks efficient per message can be expensive per sale if messages split into several segments. If SMS opt-outs start climbing, cut frequency before anything else. With SMS, fewer and better messages almost always win.
```
