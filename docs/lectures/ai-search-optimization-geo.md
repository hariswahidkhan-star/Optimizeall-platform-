Scene 2 — Two ways a model 'knows' you

```text
First, the big idea. An AI assistant can know about you in two ways. Think of an exam. A closed-book exam is training data: whatever the model absorbed before its cutoff, frozen, sometimes out of date, and not something you can edit after the fact. An open-book exam is retrieval: at answer time, the system searches an index or the live web, pulls passages, and writes from them, often with citations. AI search features lean heavily on the open-book route. And here's the key point. The open book is the web. Your web. That's where optimisation is practical.
```

Scene 3 — The retrieval pipeline

```text
So what happens inside the open-book route? Treat this as a conceptual model, because providers don't publish every detail. One: interpret the prompt, pulling out intent, entities and constraints. Two: expand it into several sub-queries. Three: retrieve candidate pages from a search index, sometimes with live fetches. Four: select passages, not whole pages. Five: generate the answer. Six: attach citations to some of the claims. Notice that steps three and four are basically search and snippet extraction. Which is why classic technical SEO still gates everything.
```

Scene 4 — Query fan-out

```text
Let's zoom into step two, because it changes how you plan content. Google calls it query fan-out. Take the prompt: best CRM for a ten-person agency in Dubai with Arabic support. The system doesn't search that sentence once. It might search CRM pricing for small teams, CRMs with Arabic interface, CRM data residency in the UAE, and agency CRM comparisons. And we have evidence this is real, not theory. Bing Webmaster Tools now shows site owners the grounding queries Copilot generated when it cited their pages. Each sub-query is a separate chance for you to be retrieved.
```

Scene 5 — Passages, not pages

```text
Now step four, passage selection. Imagine a researcher with a highlighter and five minutes per page. If your answer is buried in paragraph fourteen after a long intro about how exciting your industry is, the highlighter never reaches it. If the answer sits right under a clear heading, in two self-contained sentences with specific facts, it's easy to lift and cite. So passages matter more than pages. And one more thing. When sources disagree, systems tend to favour what several reputable sources corroborate. Your claims about yourself are weighed against what others say.
```

So for the sub-queries gluten-free, wedding, Manchester and price, there's no passage to retrieve. The fix is a plain HTML page that answers those facets. No AI trick required.
```

Scene 7 — Example 2: LedgerLite (illustrative)

```text

Scene 8 — Why answers vary

```text
Here's something that trips up a lot of teams. Generative systems are non-deterministic. Ask the same question twice and you may get different brands and different citations. Location, conversation history, model versions and index freshness all add variation. So one screenshot proves nothing, good or bad. You measure by sampling: the same prompts, several runs, on a schedule. We'll build that prompt panel properly in module five. For now, remember that citations are not rankings. They're a sample from a moving distribution.

Scene 9 — Common mistakes

```text

Google's own twenty twenty-six guidance for its AI features says it plainly: these features are rooted in core Search ranking and quality systems, and you don't need llms.txt, content chunking or special schema to take part.

Scene 1 — Entity clarity and consensus

```text
Imagine asking an assistant, what does your company do, and getting a confident answer that mixes up your founding year with a company in another country, lists a service you stopped offering two years ago, and places your head office in the wrong city. That's not a ranking problem. That's an entity problem. In this lecture you'll learn how assistants decide who you are, why consistency across the web matters so much, and how to run an entity audit that fixes it.
```

Scene 2 — What is an entity?

```text
First, what's an entity? It's a thing systems can identify and connect facts to. Your company, its products, its locations, its founders. When an assistant answers a question about you, it has to identify the right entity, then attach the right facts. Similar names, inconsistent descriptions and outdated pages cause confusion. And here's the dangerous part. Confusion doesn't always lead to silence. Sometimes it leads to confident, wrong answers about your business, delivered to a potential customer.
```

Scene 3 — The consensus principle

```text
Here's the key idea: the consensus principle. Retrieval systems see many sources. When they agree, the answer usually reflects that agreement. When they disagree, the model might pick one, hedge, or merge facts wrongly. Think of it like asking five colleagues for directions to your office. If four say third floor and one says fifth, the visitor goes to the third floor. If they all say different things, the visitor wanders. Your job is to make the truth about your business consistent and corroborated everywhere it appears.
```

Scene 4 — Where consistency lives

```text
Where does that consistency need to live? Your website: About, service pages, pricing, locations and team. Your structured data: Organization, LocalBusiness, Person and Product. Official profiles: Google Business Profile, Bing Places, Apple Business Connect, LinkedIn and social platforms. Third-party sources: directories, review sites, media coverage and industry associations. And, where legitimately applicable, reference sources like Wikipedia and Wikidata, under their own notability and conflict-of-interest rules.
```

Scene 5 — The entity fact sheet

```text
The tool that makes this manageable is an entity fact sheet. One internal document with your official name, legal entity, category, founding year, headquarters, services, industries, markets, languages, leadership, official profile links, and a one-sentence and a one-hundred-word description. Then everything else copies from it. Your About page. Your Organization markup. Your LinkedIn summary. Your directory listings. When a fact changes, you change the fact sheet first, then cascade it. That single habit prevents most entity drift.
```

Scene 6 — Example 1: Manchester clinic move

```text
Worked example one, simple. A physiotherapy clinic in Manchester moved from Deansgate to Ancoats last year. Its website and Google Business Profile are updated. But a local directory, an old press piece and its Bing Places listing still show the old address. When we ask assistants where the clinic is, two of three runs mention Deansgate. The fix list is short: update Bing Places, request corrections from the directory and the publisher with evidence, and add the move date to the About page. A month later, the old address stops appearing.
```

Scene 7 — Example 2: a name clash (illustrative)

```text
Worked example two, with illustrative details. A Karachi software house shares a similar name with a Canadian company. Assistants blend them, and the Canadian founding year shows up in answers about the Pakistani firm. The fix is disambiguation. A consistent descriptor everywhere: Karachi-based software house specialising in logistics apps. Organization markup with address, founding date and same-as links to the real profiles. An About page that states location and founding year in the first sentence. And correction requests to two directories that had merged the two companies. Re-sampling a month later shows the correct year in most runs.
```

Scene 8 — Hands-on: the entity audit

```text
Now the hands-on part. The lesson text walks you through an entity consistency audit. Collect every place your core facts appear. Record each fact per source in a sheet. Flag every mismatch against your fact sheet. Then ask three assistants four simple questions: what is the brand, where is it based, what does it offer and for whom, and who founded it. There's also a prompt that asks the assistant to list disagreements between sources. That instruction is surprisingly powerful, because it often points straight at the outdated page causing the confusion.
```

Scene 9 — Correcting misinformation

```text
What if assistants state something wrong about you? Find the source by checking the cited links and searching for the wrong fact. Fix what you control. Request corrections from third parties with evidence. Disambiguate if another entity shares your name. Use the feedback buttons most assistants provide. And be patient: search indexes refresh at different speeds, and training data only changes with new model versions. On Wikipedia, don't create or edit an article about your own business without following its conflict-of-interest rules. Earn independent coverage instead.
```

Scene 10 — Common mistakes

```text
Common mistakes. A different company description on every platform. Outdated facts, like an old office or former CEO, left on high-authority pages. Relying only on your own description without third-party corroboration. And ignoring similarly named entities until customers get confused. Every one of these is fixable with a fact sheet and a quarterly check.
```

Scene 11 — Watch me do it: a 40-minute entity audit

```text
Watch me do it. I'll run a quick entity audit for a small agency. Step one: I open a sheet with columns for source, name, category, city, phone, founded and services. Step two: I fill the first row from the website's About page and footer, then view source and check the Organization markup. The markup says founded twenty-seventeen; the About page says twenty-sixteen. First mismatch. Step three: I check LinkedIn, Google Business Profile, Bing Places, and two directories, one row each. One assistant says the agency is based in the old location and cites that directory. Now I know which source is spreading the error. Step five: I write the fixes in priority order. Correct the founding year in the markup to match the confirmed fact sheet. Update Bing Places. Email the directory with evidence. Then I set a reminder to re-ask both assistants in four weeks. Total time: about forty minutes, and every fix is traceable to a specific source.
```

Scene 12 — Make your site the best source about you

```text
Let me give you a checklist for making your own website the best source about you, because that's the part you fully control. An About page with the fact sheet information, history, leadership, and links to official profiles. Service and product pages that state capabilities explicitly, including what you don't do, which helps accurate matching. Location pages only for real offices. Pricing, or at least your pricing model. A newsroom with dated announcements. And Organization markup with a stable at-id, same-as links, logo, founders and contact points that match the visible text. If a stranger read only your site, could they describe your business accurately in two sentences? If not, neither can an assistant.
```

```text

```text

Scene 1 — Automating a prompt panel

```text
If you've tried running a prompt panel by hand, you know the problem. Fifty prompts, three runs each, four surfaces. That's six hundred checks a month, and someone's weekend. The good news is that several providers now offer APIs with built-in web search and citations, so you can run a large panel on a schedule and log it automatically. In this lecture you'll build that pipeline, turn the log into metrics, and learn the one caveat you must put in every report.
```

Scene 2 — The caveat

```text
Here's the caveat first, because it's the most important sentence in this lesson. API answers are not the same as consumer app answers. The model, the search settings, the location, personalisation and interface features can all differ. So an API panel is a proxy. Think of it like a weather station on a hill outside town. It won't tell you exactly what's happening on your street, but it's consistent, cheap and brilliant for trends. Keep a small manual sample in the real apps to calibrate it.
```

Scene 3 — Design the panel file

```text
Design the panel before you write code. Your panel file has a stable prompt ID, the market, the language, the intent, and the prompt text. Balance your intents: recommendation, comparison, problem, brand and local. Include the languages your customers use, like Arabic for Saudi Arabia or Urdu for Pakistan. And version the file. If someone quietly rewrites twenty prompts, your trends break and you won't know why.
```

```text
Now the OpenAI example in the lesson text. It uses the official Python SDK and the Responses API with the web search tool. You pass the model name through an environment variable, because supported models change, and you check OpenAI's docs for which ones support web search. Scene 5 — The loop

```text
The main loop reads the panel file, runs each prompt three times, and writes one log row per run: the date, the surface, the prompt ID, the run number, which brands were mentioned, and the cited URLs. Scene 2 — Exists today

```text
Let's be precise about what exists today. User-triggered agent fetchers are live. Google added one called Google-Agent to its crawler documentation in March twenty twenty-six, for AI agents running on Google infrastructure. OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity all have user-initiated fetchers too. Scene 3 — WebMCP (proposal)

```text
Then there's WebMCP. It's a proposal, published as a W3C draft in February twenty twenty-six and co-developed by Google and Microsoft, with an early preview and origin trial in Chrome. The idea is simple. Instead of an agent guessing what your buttons do by reading the page, your site registers named actions, like search products or get delivered price, with described inputs and outputs. Think of the difference between a tourist pointing at a menu and a waiter who hands them a clear list of dishes with prices. It's promising, but the API is still evolving, so don't ship production code from blog posts yet.
```

Scene 4 — Verifying agents

```text
One more emerging piece: verifying agents. Today, most sites identify bots by user-agent strings, which anyone can fake. Proposals for signed requests, using HTTP message signatures, sometimes called Web Bot Auth, let an agent prove cryptographically who operates it. Some providers and CDNs have started adopting this. For you, the practical upshot is that your bot management will increasingly be able to say, this really is a legitimate assistant acting for a user, and treat it accordingly.
```

Scene 5 — Readiness = accessibility + clarity

```text
Here's the reassuring part. Most agent readiness is excellent accessibility and clean engineering. Agents are demanding users. They can't guess, they don't enjoy animations, and they fail on ambiguous forms. So use semantic, accessible HTML: real buttons, links, labels and forms, with accessible names. Many agents read the accessibility tree, the same structure screen readers use, so this work also helps disabled users and your WCAG obligations. Keep flows predictable, show prices including tax and shipping, state stock clearly, and avoid dark patterns.
```

Scene 6 — Also needed

```text
Two more readiness items. Machine-readable facts: accurate JSON-LD and feeds, consistent with the page. And bot management that recognises agents. Decide which user-triggered agents you allow on which paths. Don't let a CAPTCHA wall silently block legitimate assistant visits to public product pages. But keep strong protection on login, checkout and account actions, because agents acting for users still need the user's authorisation. And write your returns, delivery times and eligibility rules as text, because agents quote them back to users.
```
