Scene 1 — How AI assistants respond to your words

```text
Have you ever asked an AI assistant for help and got back something so bland it could have been written for anyone? You are not alone, and the fix is simpler than you think. In this lecture you will learn the one idea that explains most AI behaviour, three things every user must know from day one, and a quick experiment that will change how you write every prompt from now on. No technical background needed. By the end, you will be able to predict when an assistant will give you something generic, and exactly what to add to get something you can actually use.
```

Scene 2 — The core idea

```text
Here is the idea. An AI assistant writes the response that seems most likely to fit what you gave it. It has learned patterns from an enormous amount of text: how emails flow, how recipes are laid out, how explanations are built. When your request is short and vague, it fills every gap with the most typical, average answer. When your request is clear and specific, it can tailor the answer to you. So a generic answer is not the AI being lazy. It is the AI doing its best with very little to go on. That puts the power back in your hands.
```

Scene 3 — Same assistant, two prompts

```text
Let us make that concrete. Imagine a family bakery in Lahore. The owner types: write a post about our bakery. The result talks about fresh bread and passion for baking. It could be any bakery on earth. Now she tries again: write a friendly Instagram caption, under sixty words, announcing that our family bakery now opens at seven in the morning on weekdays, so office workers can grab fresh parathas and chai before work, with one emoji and a call to visit. This time the caption is specific, on-brand and ready to post. Same assistant. Same minute. Completely different result, because she told it what a helpful friend would need to know.
```

Scene 4 — Three things to know

```text
Now, three things to remember from day one. First, the assistant does not know your situation unless you tell it. It does not know your business, your customers, your deadline or your taste. Second, it can be confidently wrong. It sometimes states facts, figures, quotes or sources that sound perfect and are simply made up. People call this hallucination. So check anything that matters, especially numbers, dates, names, and anything legal, medical or financial. Third, it is a conversation. Your first message is a starting point, not an exam. Often the best result comes from your second or third message.
```

Scene 5 — Modern assistant features

```text
Today's assistants can also do much more than answer typed questions. Many can think longer before answering hard problems, search the web and show their sources, read your PDFs and spreadsheets, look at photos and screenshots, talk with you in voice mode, remember your preferences, and even create images. We will cover all of these in module four. But here is the key point. Every one of those features still depends on the same core skill: explaining what you want clearly. A powerful assistant with a vague request still gives a vague answer.
```

Scene 6 — Your mental model

```text
A mental model that will serve you throughout this course: treat the assistant like a brilliant new colleague on their first day. They are fast, well read and eager to help. But they know nothing about your organisation, and they will not ask questions unless you invite them to. Whenever an answer disappoints you, ask yourself one question. What would a smart new colleague have needed to know to do this well? The fix is almost always hiding in the answer to that question.
```

Scene 7 — Try it now

```text
Try this experiment right now. Pause the video if you like. Ask your assistant: give me tips for a job interview. Read the answer. Then ask again, with details: I have a video interview on Thursday for a junior marketing role at a skincare brand. I am nervous about the question tell me about yourself. Give me a sixty-second answer structure and a sample answer, based on one year running social media for a family restaurant where I grew followers from eight hundred to three thousand. Compare the two answers. The second one is useful because you gave it real material to work with. No secret words, no tricks.
```

Scene 8 — The honesty test

```text
One more quick test tells you a lot about any assistant. Ask it: what were the three biggest news stories this morning? If it is not searching the web, a good assistant admits it cannot know. If it confidently lists stories with no sources, you have just seen hallucination in action. Keep that in mind whenever you ask about anything recent.
```

Scene 9 — Three beginner reactions to replace

```text
Let us look at three common beginner reactions, and what to do instead. Reaction one: the AI is useless, it gave me something generic. Instead, ask what a new colleague would have needed, and add it. Reaction two: the AI said it confidently, so it must be right. Instead, treat names, numbers, dates and quotes as leads to check. Reaction three: I'll start again from scratch every time. Instead, reply in the same chat and steer: shorter, warmer, add the deadline. Each of these small shifts makes the next answer better, and together they change how useful AI feels in your day.
```

Scene 10 — Example 1: naming a cat

```text
Let us slow down and walk through a simple example, step by step. Imagine you ask: suggest a name for my cat. You get ten generic names. Luna. Milo. Oliver. Nothing wrong, nothing special. Now add three facts. She is a grey rescue cat from Karachi, she is very dramatic, and I love old Bollywood films. Suddenly the list changes. You get names inspired by classic film stars, with a line explaining each one. What changed? Not the assistant. Not some secret wording. You simply gave it material to work with. Here is the key idea: every detail you add narrows the range of likely answers, and moves the result from average toward yours.
```

Scene 11 — Example 2: a payment reminder (illustrative numbers)

```text
Now a realistic business scenario. The numbers here are illustrative. Imran runs a small printing shop in Lahore with about forty regular business customers. He needs a reminder message for customers with unpaid invoices. His first prompt: write a payment reminder. The result is stiff and could offend loyal clients. His second prompt gives context: the customer is a long-standing client, the invoice is for twelve thousand rupees, it is fifteen days overdue, the tone must stay warm because we want repeat orders, send it on WhatsApp, under sixty words, and offer a bank transfer or a pickup payment. The second message is polite, specific and ready to send. If even a quarter of those forty customers pay a few days sooner, that one improved prompt is worth real cash flow. Same tool. Better briefing.
```

Scene 12 — Recap and next step

```text
Let us recap. The assistant writes the most likely fitting answer to what it sees, so vague in means generic out. It only knows what you tell it, it can be confidently wrong, and it works best as a conversation. Think of it as a brilliant new colleague who needs a proper brief. Try this now: pick one task you did this week, an email, a post or a plan, and write two prompts for it, a one-liner and a detailed version with audience, purpose and real details. Compare the answers. In the next lesson, we will break a great prompt into five building blocks you can use every single time.
```

Scene 1 — The anatomy of a good prompt

```text
What if every good prompt you ever wrote had the same hidden structure? It does. In this lecture you will learn five building blocks that turn a vague request into one the AI can nail first time. You will watch a prompt grow block by block, see a complete worked example, and leave with a ten-second self-check you can run before pressing enter. By the end, you will never stare at an empty chat box wondering what to type.
```

Scene 2 — T-CAFE

```text
Here are the five blocks. Task: what you want done, starting with a clear action verb like write, summarise, compare or plan. Context: the background, who you are, the situation and the goal. Audience: who the result is for and what they already know. Format: length, structure, tone and language. And Examples or constraints: a sample of what good looks like, plus rules about what to include or avoid. A handy memory aid is T CAFE. Task, Context, Audience, Format, Examples. You will not need every block every time. But knowing them means you are never stuck.
```

Scene 3 — Building block by block

```text
Watch a prompt grow. We start with the task only: write an email to my landlord. The AI has no idea why. Add context: ask them to fix the heater in my flat, it stopped working five days ago, and I reported it by phone on Monday. Now add audience: my landlord is polite but slow, and I want to keep a good relationship. Add format: under one hundred and fifty words, polite but firm, with a clear request for a repair date by Friday, and a subject line. Finally, constraints: do not threaten legal action, and mention that I have a young child at home. Five or six sentences, and the email needs only minor edits.
```

Scene 4 — Each missing block = a guess

```text
Why does each block matter? Without a clear task, the AI may explain when you wanted it to write. Without context, it invents a generic situation. Without an audience, it guesses the tone and depth, and an explanation for a ten-year-old is very different from one for a finance director. Without format, you get whatever length is typical, usually too long. And without examples or constraints, it cannot match your style or avoid the things you dislike. Each missing block is a gap the AI fills with an average guess.
```

Scene 5 — Label longer prompts

```text
For longer prompts, especially when you paste material in, labels help. Write TASK, then CONTEXT, then AUDIENCE, then FORMAT, then paste the material between clear markers like three dashes. The labels help you remember the parts, and they help the AI tell your instructions apart from the text you want it to work on. Imagine a phone repair shop owner in Dubai replying to a two-star review. Task: write a reply. Context: the customer waited two hours because a part was delayed, and we gave a ten percent discount. Audience: the customer, and future customers reading reviews. Format: under eighty words, warm and professional. Constraints: apologise sincerely, no excuses, invite them back.
```

Scene 6 — Role: seasoning, not the meal

```text
You may have seen advice to start every prompt with you are a world-class marketer, or you are an expert career coach. A role like that can help set tone and depth. But a role on its own is not magic. You are a world-class marketer, write a post, still has no context, no audience and no format. Think of the role as seasoning, not the meal. Add it when it helps, and never instead of the five blocks.
```

Scene 7 — The ten-second self-check

```text
Here is your ten-second self-check before you press enter. Would a smart stranger understand what I want? Did I say who it is for? Did I say how long and in what format? And is there anything I would be annoyed to see? If so, say it up front. A great way to test this is the stranger test: show only your prompt to a friend. If they could do the task without asking you a single question, your prompt is ready.
```

Scene 8 — Practice round: a booking confirmation

```text
Here is a quick practice round. A freelance photographer in Karachi needs to confirm a wedding booking on WhatsApp. Task: write the confirmation message. Context: the couple paid a thirty percent deposit, and the shoot covers the mehndi and the baraat. Audience: the bride's elder sister, who is organising everything and very busy. Format: under seventy words, warm and professional, English with one Urdu greeting, line breaks and no bullet points. Constraints: mention that the balance is due one week before the first event, and do not mention prices. Notice how quickly the blocks fall into place once you know them. That is the whole point of a structure.
```


Scene 1 — Giving context and examples

```text
The single biggest upgrade most people can make to their AI results is not a clever phrase. It is context. In this lecture you will learn what context the AI actually needs, how to paste material safely, how to use examples to show the style you want, and how modern assistants let you store context once so you stop repeating yourself. By the end, you will know how to diagnose a bad answer by spotting what the AI did not know.
```

Scene 2 — What context covers

```text
Imagine asking a colleague to draft a reply to this client. A good colleague would ask: who is the client, what happened, what do we want to happen next, and is anything sensitive? The AI needs the same information, but it will not ask unless you invite it. Useful context covers five things. Who you are. The situation. Your goal. Your constraints. And any relevant material, like the email thread or your price list. A good rule of thumb: include anything that would change the answer. Your company's founding year probably would not. Your refund policy probably would.
```

Scene 3 — Paste real material, safely

```text
Often the best context is the actual document. Paste the client's email, your notes or the product sheet, and label each piece clearly, for example: here is the client's email, then the email between dashes, then here is our price list, then the list between dashes. Labels help the AI separate your instructions from your material. But before you paste, pause. Remove anything private you would not want shared, such as personal phone numbers, ID numbers, passwords or confidential figures, and follow your workplace rules about which AI tools are approved.
```

Scene 4 — Show, don't just tell



Scene 6 — Prompting the modes

```text
How do you prompt a thinking mode? Give it all the information up front, because it can reason but it cannot invent facts you did not supply. Say what done looks like, such as a table with these columns and a one-line recommendation. And do not micromanage the steps. You do not need to write think step by step; the mode already does that. For deep research, state the decision the research supports, the scope, the type of sources to prefer, and the output you want. Many research tools ask clarifying questions first. Answer them properly; that is your best moment to steer.
```

Scene 7 — Example 1: a dinner for eight

```text
Let us make the choice concrete with a simple example. You are planning a dinner for eight friends, two vegetarians and one with a nut allergy, on a budget of eighty pounds. Quick mode can suggest dishes instantly. But if you ask for a full menu, a shopping list with quantities, and a total cost that stays under budget, you now have constraints that must all hold at once. That is when a thinking mode earns its extra seconds. It is more likely to keep the allergy, the vegetarians and the budget straight. And if you ask, which supermarket has the best price on salmon this week, that needs current information, so switch on web search.
```

Scene 8 — Example 2: a free-delivery threshold (illustrative)

```text

```

Scene 9 — Four traps

```text
Watch for four traps. Using thinking mode for everything and hitting your usage limits by Wednesday. Treating a long, confident research report as verified truth; open the sources. Forgetting that a quick mode without web access may not know recent events. And assuming the newest or most expensive mode is always best. Often it is not; the right mode is the one that matches the job.
```

After a week of making this choice deliberately, it becomes automatic.

```text
Your assistant can read a forty-page contract, decode a blurry photo of a receipt, and hold a spoken conversation while you walk. But only if you ask the right way. Scene 2 — Why multimodal prompting?
```

```text
Why does this matter? Because retyping is slow and error-prone, and a lot of the information you need is already sitting in a PDF, a photo or your own voice. An assistant that can read the actual document works from the real source, not your summary of it. But there is a catch. It can also misread a blurry number or miss a line on page thirty. Scene 3 — Prompting with files
```

```text
Start with files. When you upload a PDF, a document or a spreadsheet, say what it is and why you shared it. This is our staff handbook, and I need to answer employee questions about annual leave. ```text
```

```text
The AI said so is not a source. But the AI found these three official pages, and I checked them, is. Scene 2 — Why sources matter
```

```text
Why does this matter? Because decisions increasingly start with an AI answer, and an answer without sources is just a confident opinion. Prices change. Rules change. Products change. A research answer with citations lets you trace every important claim back to where it came from, in minutes. Think of the assistant as a fast research intern: brilliant at gathering and summarising, but you still read the key pages before you sign anything.
```

Scene 3 — When to use which

```text
Ordinary chat answers come from what the model learned during training, which can be out of date or simply wrong. Web search lets the assistant read current pages and cite them. Deep research goes further: it plans a series of searches, reads many sources over several minutes, and writes a long, structured report with citations. Most major assistants offer versions of both, with different names and limits by plan. Use web search for a quick current fact. Use deep research for comparisons and overviews across many sources. Use normal chat for concepts that do not change. And for your own documents, upload them instead of searching.
```

Scene 4 — The research prompt

```text
A research prompt needs more direction than a chat prompt, because the assistant makes many choices for you. Use four parts. Goal: the decision this supports, like choosing an email marketing tool for a UK shop with three thousand subscribers. Question: the criteria, such as monthly price, ease of use, Shopify integration, automation and UK and EU data handling. Sources: prefer vendors' official pricing pages and recent independent reviews, and ignore affiliate listicles. Output: a comparison table, a short recommendation, a list of sources with dates, and a flag on any price that could not be confirmed on an official page.
```

Scene 5 — Five-step source check

```text
Research reports look authoritative. They are long, tidy and full of links, which makes them easy to over-trust. So here is a five-step check. Three: look for missing viewpoints; did it only read vendor pages, or skip an important option? ```text
```
```text
Why does this matter? Because most businesses sit on feedback they never read properly: reviews, surveys, support emails, sales notes. Scene 3 — Analysis patterns
```

```text
Analysis patterns first. Summary for a reader: summarise this for my manager who has two minutes, focusing on what matters to them. Key points and gaps: list the key points, then the important questions it does not answer. Compare: put option A and option B in a table by cost, effort, risks and benefits. ```
```

Scene 4 — More analysis patterns

```text
Three more analysis patterns are worth knowing. The extract: pull every date, name, price or action into a list, marking anything unclear as unclear. The devil's advocate: here is my plan, argue against it as strongly as you can. And the decision helper: I am deciding between these options, my priorities in order are these, ask me questions, then recommend one and explain why. That last one is a great fit for a thinking mode, because it juggles several priorities at once.
```

Scene 5 — When to think harder

```text
When should you switch on a thinking mode for analysis? When the question has several steps, like comparing three suppliers on price, delivery and risk before recommending one. When numbers must be combined, like working out a break-even point. And when you want a plan challenged properly. For quick theme-finding or simple summaries, the normal mode is usually enough. And if your assistant can run code on an uploaded spreadsheet, ask it to calculate, not estimate, and check that it did.
```

Scene 6 — Brainstorming patterns

```text
Now brainstorming, where AI's speed shines. Quantity first, then filter. The big list: give me twenty ideas, one line each, including some unusual ones. The constraint twist: ten ideas under a set budget that can be done this month. The perspective shift: how would a teenager, a busy parent or a competitor see this? Build on mine: here are my three ideas, give three variations each, then three in a new direction. Then filter and rank: pick the best five for my criteria. And finally, the first step: what is the smallest, cheapest test I could run this week?
```

Scene 7 — Better spread

```text
AI brainstorms can feel samey. Three fixes help. Ask for ideas in named categories, like cheap, bold, digital and community. Ask for three ideas most businesses in your industry would dismiss. And set a hard constraint, such as nothing that needs a budget above five hundred pounds. Constraints and categories force variety.
```

Scene 1 — Checking AI work

```text
A marketing assistant in Jeddah almost sent a launch email promising free delivery across the whole Gulf, when the offer only covered Saudi Arabia. Five minutes of checking saved the company thousands of awkward customer conversations. In this lecture you will learn a four-point check for every AI output, how to verify answers that come with sources, the privacy settings worth checking today, and how to build a personal prompt toolkit. By the end, you will move fast and stay trustworthy.
```

Scene 2 — Why check?

```text
Why does checking matter so much? Because you are responsible for what you send, publish or decide, not the AI. A wrong price, a biased phrase or a leaked customer detail carries your name, not the model's. The good news is that checking does not have to be slow. A consistent routine of a few minutes catches most problems, and it lets you use AI confidently rather than nervously. Think of it as the final quality check on a production line: quick, routine and non-negotiable.
```

Scene 3 — The four-point check

```text
Here is the four-point check. One: did it do what I asked? Right task, audience, length and format. Two: is it true? Check facts, names, numbers, dates, quotes and sources that matter. Three: is it appropriate? Tone, sensitivity, fairness, and anything that could embarrass you, offend someone or break a rule. Four: is it mine? Does it sound like you or your brand, and would you put your name on it? Four questions, a few minutes, every time something matters.
```

Scene 4 — Checking facts efficiently

```text
To check facts efficiently, ask the assistant to flag uncertainty. Try: review your answer before I use it; list every factual claim; say whether each came from a source I gave you, a web source you cited, or your general knowledge; and mark the three claims I should verify first. It will not catch everything, but it points you to the riskiest parts. Then use the right tool for numbers, a calculator or spreadsheet, not your eyes on an AI answer.
```

Scene 5 — Checking sourced answers

```text
Answers with citations need their own routine. Open at least two of the cited sources and confirm they actually say what the answer claims. Check the dates, because prices, rules and schedules change. Check the type of source: an official government or company page outranks a forum post or an old blog. And ask what would change the answer, and what it did not find. Sources can be misquoted, outdated, or attached to sentences they do not support.
```

Scene 6 — Appropriateness and fairness

```text
Fairness matters too. AI can reflect stereotypes found in its training text. When writing about people, such as job adverts, customer personas or performance feedback, read carefully for unfair assumptions about gender, age, nationality, religion or disability. And for advertising, the rules on honest claims and disclosure of sponsored content apply to AI-written text exactly as they do to anything else.
```

Scene 7 — Privacy settings to check today

```text
Take five minutes today to open your assistant's settings. Look for whether your chats may be used to improve the provider's models, and how to opt out if you want to. Look at memory, what is remembered, and how to delete it. Check how long chats are kept. And find out whether your organisation requires a business or enterprise plan for work data. Data protection laws such as the UK GDPR, the UAE's federal data protection law and Saudi Arabia's personal data protection law put duties on organisations that handle personal data. Follow your organisation's AI policy.
```

Scene 8 — Your prompt toolkit

```text
The best prompters are not people who memorise tricks. They are people who save what works. Build a simple toolkit document: a name for each prompt, the prompt itself, when to use it, and what to check. For example, weekly team update, customer complaint reply, learning a new tool. Update it whenever a prompt works especially well, and move the stable parts into custom instructions or projects so you do not repeat them.
```

Scene 9 — A reusable checking prompt

```text
Here is a reusable checking prompt you can send before using any important answer. Review your answer above before I use it. First, list every factual claim, numbers, dates, names and rules, as bullets. Second, for each, say whether it came from a source I gave you, a web source you cited, or your general knowledge. Third, mark the three claims I should verify first, and explain why. Do not rewrite the answer yet. It will not catch everything, but it tells you exactly where to look first.
```

Scene 10 — Example 1: a retirement speech

```text
A simple example of the four-point check. You ask for a short speech for your father's retirement. Did it do what I asked? It is four minutes, you wanted two, so ask for a shorter version. Is it true? It says he worked there for twenty-five years, and it was twenty-two, so fix it. Is it appropriate? One joke might embarrass him in front of his manager, so remove it. Is it mine? It says esteemed colleagues, which you would never say, so change it. Four questions, two minutes, and the speech is right in every way that matters.
```

Scene 11 — Example 2: job adverts (illustrative)

```text
Now a business scenario, with illustrative numbers. A recruitment agency in Riyadh uses AI to draft job adverts, about forty a month. Before posting, the team runs the four-point check. One advert asked for young and energetic candidates. That is an age-related phrase that creates discrimination risk, so it becomes motivated and eager to learn. Another listed a salary range that did not match the client's brief. The team asks the assistant to list every factual claim with its origin, and verifies salary, location and benefits against the client form. It adds about five minutes per advert. Across forty adverts, that is a little over three hours a month, a small price for avoiding a discriminatory advert or a salary dispute. Illustrative figures.
```

Scene 12 — Recap and next steps

```text
To recap. Run the four-point check on anything that matters. Ask the assistant to list and rank its claims, open the sources behind sourced answers, and use proper tools for numbers. Watch for bias, follow privacy rules and be open about AI use where required. And build a toolkit of prompts that work. Try this now: create your toolkit document with at least five prompts you will reuse, each with a note on what to check. You now have the foundations. Keep practising on real tasks; that is where the skill grows.
```
