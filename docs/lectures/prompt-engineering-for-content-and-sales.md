Scene 1 — Techniques for better thinking

```text
Once you've mastered structure and refinement, there's a next level: getting the AI to think better, not just write better. Better campaign ideas. Sharper diagnoses of why a post flopped. Stronger arguments in a sales email. In this lecture you'll learn seven techniques, none of them technical, including reasoning before answering, generating many ideas then filtering, role playing your audience, and chaining big tasks. You'll also learn when to switch on reasoning and deep research modes. Scene 2 — Why techniques matter
```

```text
Why bother? Three problems. First, the first ideas a model produces tend to be the most predictable, the same ones everyone else is getting. Second, when a model jumps straight to a recommendation, you can't see or challenge its reasoning. And third, big one shot tasks, like write a full launch campaign, let errors compound silently: a wrong assumption in step one ruins everything after it. Each technique in this lecture fixes one of those problems. Together, they turn the assistant from a fast typist into a useful thinking partner.
```

Scene 3 — Reason first, then answer

```text
Technique one: ask for reasoning before the answer. Before recommending a campaign angle, analyze the audience's main motivations and objections, then recommend. You'll see the logic, and you can challenge it. Many assistants also have thinking or reasoning modes that spend more time working through a problem. Use them for positioning decisions, diagnosing underperformance with your real data, or planning a content series. Don't bother for captions. And when the thinking needs current external facts, like a competitor's positioning, switch to deep research instead. A longer answer isn't automatically a better one, so always read the reasoning critically.
```

Scene 4 — Many, then filter

```text
Technique two: generate many, then filter. Give me twenty five hook ideas, then pick the five strongest for a skeptical audience and explain why. Scene 2 — Why one-shot long-form fails
```

```text
Why does one shot long form fail? Three reasons. Structure drifts: without an agreed outline, the model decides the shape, and it usually picks the most common shape, a list of tips. Filler fills space: when you ask for a word count without enough substance, the model pads. And facts get invented: the longer the piece, the more places a plausible but unsupported claim can hide. The fix is to split the job so you can steer. You approve the angle. You fix the outline. The model drafts sections from your notes. Separate passes clean it up. It sounds slower. ```
```

Scene 3 — The building-site analogy


Scene 2 — Why discovery decides deals

```text
Why does discovery matter so much? Because deals are won or lost on understanding. The salesperson who understands the buyer's situation, the impact of the problem and how the decision gets made can shape a proposal that fits. There's a second benefit people miss. The buyer's own words, their description of the pain, become your best sales copy, in the follow up, the proposal, and even your marketing. And third, speed after the call builds trust. ```text
```

Scene 2 — Why it matters

```text
Why does this matter? Because sameness is invisible. If your content sounds like everyone else's, it doesn't matter how well it's written. Here's the key idea. Your competitive advantage is whatever the model can't know: your experiences, your customers, your opinions and your data. The model has read the internet. It hasn't sat in your client meetings, answered your customers' messages or made your mistakes. Specific, true details build trust, because they're hard to fake and easy to recognize.
```

Scene 3 — The tells

```text
Learn to spot the tells. Stock openers, like in today's fast paced world, or are you ready to take your X to the next level. Overused words: elevate, unlock, seamless, game changer, delve, landscape, journey, empower. Everything in groups of three, with every paragraph the same length. Vague benefits, like boost your productivity, without a concrete example. A neat moral at the end of every post. And no opinion, no tension, no specifics. Once you see these, you can't unsee them. And neither can your audience.
```

Scene 4 — The home-cooked meal analogy

```text
Think of generic AI content as a supermarket ready meal. It's convenient, it's fine, and it tastes exactly like everyone else's. Original content is a home cooked meal. The recipe might be simple, but the ingredients are yours: the story from last Tuesday, the customer's exact words, your opinion that goes against the grain, the number from your own dashboard. And the seasoning is yours too: your rhythm, your jokes, the rough edges you keep. AI can help you cook. But if you only give it supermarket ingredients, you'll get a ready meal.
```
