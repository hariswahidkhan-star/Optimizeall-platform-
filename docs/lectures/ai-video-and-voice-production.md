Scene 1 — How AI voice works

```text
Close your eyes for a second and imagine the last ad you heard on a podcast. Could you tell whether that voice was a person or a model? In twenty twenty-six, often you can't. In this lecture you'll learn how modern AI voices actually work, how to read a vendor's model menu, and how to pick and test a voice so that every video you make sounds consistent. Scene 2 — Why it matters

```text
Why does this matter? Because the voice is the part of a video people forgive least. Viewers will tolerate simple visuals, but a voice that mispronounces your brand, drifts in tone halfway through, or sounds flat will make them scroll. And for creators and small teams, voice is also where AI saves the most time. A voiceover that used to need a booth, a narrator and a retake session can now be generated, corrected and re-rendered in minutes. The skill is no longer recording. It's directing.
```

Scene 3 — The core idea

```text
Here's the core idea. Old text-to-speech glued together tiny recorded fragments, which is why it sounded choppy. Modern neural models learned from huge amounts of recorded speech, so they generate audio directly, including breath, rhythm and emotion. Think of it like a session musician who has heard thousands of songs. You don't hand them individual notes. You hand them a chart and a feel, and they perform. Your script is the chart. Punctuation, sentence length and a few cues are the feel. The model fills in the rest.
```

Scene 4 — Reading the model menu

```text
Now let's read a real model menu. As of September twenty twenty-six, ElevenLabs lists three main text-to-speech models. Eleven v3 is the expressive one, great for ads, stories and dialogue, and it understands little stage directions called audio tags, like whispers or excited. Multilingual v2 is the steady one, best for long narration where every paragraph should sound the same. Flash two point five is the fast one, built for live agents where speed beats polish. The names will change. The pattern won't: expressive, stable, fast. Pick for the job.
```

Scene 5 — The four dials

```text
Next, the settings you'll meet. Exact names vary, but most tools give you four dials. Stability controls how even the delivery is. Higher means consistent, lower means more emotion and more surprises. Similarity controls how closely the output sticks to the original voice. Style exaggerates the voice's character, and too much sounds theatrical. And speed is pace. Here's the discipline that separates pros from hobbyists. Start with the defaults. Change one dial at a time. Render the same paragraph after each change and compare them side by side. Otherwise you'll never know which change actually helped.
```

Scene 6 — Example 1: a 30-second teaser

```text
First example, a simple one. You need a thirty-second product teaser. You paste the script into the voice tool, choose a library voice, and generate. Then you break the last sentence in two and add a full stop, which gives the voice a beat to breathe. Generate again. Same voice, same model, and now it lands. Notice you changed the text, not the settings.
```

Scene 10 — Common mistakes

```text

```text

If people mention the voice, good or bad, that's data. A falling rework rate and steady completion rates tell you your voice system is healthy.

Scene 12 — Recap

```text

```text

Stock avatars are pre-made presenters, quick but shared with other brands. Custom or digital twin avatars are made from footage of a real person, usually you, with their consent recorded. Photo avatars animate a still image. And generated avatars are fictional presenters created from a prompt. Platforms such as HeyGen add templates, brand kits and video translation with lip-sync on top.
```

Scene 4 — The principle

```text
Here's the principle I want you to remember. Use the avatar for information. Use the human for connection. Think of an avatar like a well-designed airport announcement. Clear, consistent, available in several languages, and nobody expects it to feel personal. But when something goes wrong with your flight, you want to speak to a person at the desk. Training, onboarding, how-to videos and routine product updates are announcements. Apologies, crisis responses, personal stories and testimonials are desk conversations.
```

Scene 5 — The overlooked option

```text
Now a format many teams overlook: a voice over animated slides. No face at all. Just a clear voice, diagrams, code or screen recordings, and key ideas building on screen. For teaching, it's often the best of both worlds. There's no uncanny face to distract anyone, it's easy to update, and the visual channel is free to show exactly what's being explained. In fact, that's how the lectures in this academy are produced. So before you pick an avatar, ask whether a face actually adds anything.
```

Scene 6 — Creating a good twin

```text
If you do create a custom twin, the input footage decides almost everything. Follow the platform's recording guidance, but the fundamentals are universal. Consent first: the person records any required consent statement and signs your internal form covering scope, duration and deletion. Then a quiet, well-lit space with soft, even light on the face and a simple background. A stable camera at eye level, framed the way you want the final videos. Relaxed, natural delivery with neutral gestures. Solid-colored clothing, because fine patterns shimmer. And a short test with tricky words before you produce anything real.
```

```text

Scene 10 — Scoring the test

```text
Then I score it from one to five on six things. Lip-sync on the letters p, b and m, where mouths must close. Eye contact. Hand and gesture naturalness. Pronunciation. Skin and teeth artifacts. And consistency between two takes of the same line. My pass rule is four or more on a phone and at least three on a laptop. If it fails, I fix the input first: better lighting in the source footage, a cleaner audio track, shorter sentences. Only then do I blame the platform.
```

Scene 11 — Personalized video at scale

```text
One more format deserves care: personalized video at scale. Avatar tools can generate hundreds of variations, each addressing a prospect by name. That can lift relevance, but handle it carefully. Use only data the recipient would expect you to have, and follow data protection and anti-spam rules in each market. Don't imply you personally recorded a message when you didn't. And test a handful before sending hundreds, because one wrong name or mispronounced company in a batch of five hundred becomes a screenshot. Personalization should feel helpful, never creepy.
```

```text
Common mistakes. Using a stock avatar as a fake customer giving a testimonial. That's deceptive and likely breaks advertising rules. Break content into short scenes with b-roll and on-screen text. And personalized videos at scale that imply you recorded a message personally when you didn't. A small note, made with my AI avatar, keeps trust intact.
```

```text

Treat personalized video at scale with extra care: expected data only, no fake personal recordings, and a small test batch first. And whenever an avatar presents, tell your audience. Disclosure costs you almost nothing and protects the trust you're trying to build.
```

```

Scene 7 — The scene table

```text
The format that holds it all together is the scene table. Columns for scene, seconds, voiceover, on-screen text and visual. Why a table? Because it forces you to decide what the viewer sees for every line the voice says. It keeps the voice producer, the editor and the reviewer looking at the same thing. And it maps directly onto per-scene audio files, which makes fixes cheap. If a line has no visual, that's a warning. If a visual has no line, it's probably b-roll, which is fine, as long as you planned it.
```

Same meaning, but now an AI voice can deliver it naturally, and a viewer can follow it without rewinding.
```

```text

```text

```text

Forgetting to test pronunciation of local names like places, dishes and people. Letting the AI add claims beyond your facts. And skipping the read-aloud. Here's a simple test: if you stumble, the AI voice will sound off too. Your own mouth is the fastest quality check you have.
```

Scene 1 — End-to-end production workflow

```text
A client once asked an agency a simple question: who approved the script for last month's video, which voice did you use, and do you have the consent for it? Scene 2 — Why a pipeline

```text
Why a pipeline instead of just making videos? Because AI tools make individual steps fast, which tempts people to skip the steps between them. The script goes to voice before anyone checks the facts. A generated shot goes into the edit before anyone checks for artifacts. The video goes live before anyone applies a label. Each shortcut saves ten minutes and occasionally costs a client. A pipeline puts the checks in fixed places, so speed doesn't come at the price of mistakes.
```

Scene 3 — The restaurant pass

```text
Here's the analogy. Think of a restaurant pass. Different stations cook different parts of the dish, but everything goes through one place where the head chef checks it before it leaves the kitchen. In video, specialist tools are the stations: a voice platform, a video generator, an avatar tool, an editor. The pipeline is the route between them, and the gates are where a person checks the work. The best professional pattern generates voice in a dedicated tool, then drives the video with that approved audio.
```

Scene 4 — Seven stages

```text
Here are the seven stages. One, brief and script: audience, goal, platform, length, languages, then the scene table, with facts approved. Four, captions. Five, review and quality control, including native speakers and stakeholders. Six, localize. Seven, publish and label, with platform AI labels and sponsorship disclosures where they apply.
```

Scene 5 — Two gates

```text
And the two gates that matter most. Gate one sits between script and voice: nothing gets voiced until a named person has approved the script and the facts. Gate two sits before publishing: nothing goes live until quality control has passed and labels are applied. Why these two? Because they protect the most expensive mistakes. Voicing and editing an unapproved script wastes hours. Publishing without checks or labels risks reputation, platform penalties and client trust. Everything else can be fixed later. These two can't.

Scene 1 — Captions, accessibility and QC

```text
Here's a habit that should change how you edit: on social platforms, lots of people watch with the sound off, at least at first. You've probably done it yourself, on a train, in a waiting room, or late at night next to someone sleeping. If your video doesn't work silently, it doesn't work. And for viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing, captions aren't a preference, they're access. In this lecture you'll learn a captioning workflow, accessibility practices beyond captions, and a quality-control checklist built for AI media.
```

Scene 2 — Why it's quality

```text
Why is this part of quality, not an optional extra? Three reasons. Accessibility: captions and good narration make your content usable by more people, and accessibility laws and public-sector requirements in several markets reinforce this. Reach: sound-off viewing is common, and non-native speakers rely on captions to follow. And risk: AI media fails in small, specific ways, like a mispronounced name, a lip-sync glitch, or a morphing hand. A structured QC pass is what catches those before your audience does.
```

Scene 3 — The pre-flight mindset

```text
Here's the mental model for QC. Think of an airline pre-flight checklist. Pilots don't skip it because they're experienced. They use it because experience makes you confident, and confidence makes you skip things. AI media is the same. After fifty videos, you'll feel sure the captions are fine and the pronunciation is right. The checklist is there for the day they aren't. It covers four areas: audio, visuals, content and compliance, and ends with a final phone check.
```

Scene 4 — Captioning workflow

```text
Let's start with the captioning workflow. First, auto-generate captions in your editor, avatar platform or a transcription tool. Second, correct them, because automatic captions often get names, brand terms, numbers, local words and mixed languages like Urdu and English wrong. Third, style them: high contrast, big enough for mobile, one or two lines, broken at natural phrases, and positioned away from faces and platform buttons. Fourth, check timing. Fifth, caption each language version in its own language. And sixth, upload caption files where platforms support them.
```

Scene 5 — Why caption files

```text
Why upload a caption file when captions are already burned in? Because a separate file, usually an SRT, lets viewers turn captions on or off, lets platforms index your words, and lets you fix a typo without re-exporting the whole video. An SRT is simple: a number, a start and end time, and one or two lines of text, repeated. On YouTube, for example, you can upload one per language. Burned-in captions are still great for social feeds where autoplay is silent. Often you'll want both.
```

Scene 6 — Beyond captions

```text
Accessibility goes beyond captions. Describe key visuals in the narration when they carry meaning. Instead of saying as you can see on the chart, sales doubled, say sales doubled from March to June. Avoid rapid flashing effects that can harm people with photosensitive conditions. Keep strong color contrast for on-screen text. Use plain language, which helps everyone, including viewers using translation tools. And add alt text to thumbnails and images in posts where platforms support it. Ask yourself: would this make sense to someone who can't hear it, and to someone who can't see it?
```

Scene 7 — QC checklist, part 1

```text
Now the QC checklist itself. Audio: every word pronounced correctly, no glitches or sudden volume jumps, a consistent voice across scenes, and licensed music that doesn't overpower speech. Visuals: accurate lip-sync, no distorted hands, faces, text or morphing objects, correct brand colors and fonts, and no unintended recognizable people, logos or sensitive locations. Content: facts, prices and claims match approved sources, localized versions reviewed by native speakers, cultural sensitivities considered, and generated visuals illustrate but never prove.
```

Scene 8 — QC checklist, part 2

```text
And compliance. Consent on file for any cloned voice or custom avatar. Platform AI-content labels applied where required. Sponsorship disclosures present where applicable. Captions accurate in each language. Then the final line, which is my favorite: watched in full, on a phone, with sound on and with sound off. Why a phone with sound off? Because that's how many short-form viewers watch. This one pass reveals captions hidden under platform buttons, text too small to read, and whether the video still makes sense silently.
```

```text
First example, a simple one. A Dubai fitness studio's AI-dubbed Arabic promo passes the audio check. Then the sound-off phone test reveals two problems. The captions are still in English, and they sit under the like and comment buttons. The team regenerates Arabic captions, corrects two misspelled class names, moves the captions up, and publishes. That ten-minute check prevented a confusing launch, and it only happened because the checklist said phone, sound off.
```

Scene 10 — Example 2: UK public-sector training

```text
Second example, a business case. A UK training company that sells to public-sector clients adopts a caption standard: sentence case, two lines, speaker labels when there are two voices, and sound cues like music fades where they matter. They add an accessibility statement to each course page. And their QC checklist gains one line: would this make sense to someone who can't hear it, and to someone who can't see it? That question keeps catching narration that says look at this without describing what this is.
```

Scene 1 — The marketing video playbook

```text
What if, instead of making one perfect ad, you made nine good ones in the time it used to take to make one, and let your audience tell you which works? That's the real superpower of AI in marketing video. Not one beautiful asset, but a testing system. In this lecture you'll learn which formats suit AI production, how to plan variants that actually teach you something, how to write a fifteen-second vertical ad with a shot list, and how to measure results honestly.
```

Scene 2 — Why a system

```text
Why think in systems? Because marketing is uncertain. Even experienced teams can't reliably predict which hook, voice or length will work for a new audience. Traditional production made testing expensive, so teams guessed and hoped. AI makes variants cheap, which means you can replace guessing with evidence. But cheap variants also make it easy to test sloppily, or to cross ethical lines with fake testimonials. The system keeps both speed and integrity.
```

Scene 3 — The chef's test

```text
Here's the mental model. Think of a chef testing a new dish. They don't change the recipe, the plating and the portion size all at once, because then they'd never know what diners liked. They change one thing, serve it, and watch. A variant plan works the same way. You keep a control version, and each variant changes one variable: the hook, the voice, the length, or the language. Everything else stays constant, including the real product shots and the approved offer text.
```

Scene 4 — Formats that suit AI

```text
Which formats suit AI production? Product explainers, where AI helps with voice, concept b-roll and localization, but the product interface and results come from real screen recordings. Vertical ads, where AI helps with hook and voice variants, captions and sizes, but prices and offers are real. Localized campaigns, with dubbing and native review. And founder or expert messages, where AI can help with script and audio cleanup, but the person should be filmed for real, because that format runs on trust.
```

Scene 5 — UGC-style ads

```text
And one format needs a clear warning: so-called U G C style ads, which imitate user-generated content. AI can help script and edit them. But AI-generated customers or reviews presented as real people are deceptive. The US Federal Trade Commission's rule on fake reviews and testimonials took effect in October twenty twenty-four, and the UK's advertising regulator takes fake testimonials seriously too. Use real creators, disclose paid partnerships, and label AI where required. There's no variant plan clever enough to justify a fake customer.
```

Scene 6 — A variant plan

```text
Now the variant plan itself, using a Ramadan meal-kit campaign for Saudi Arabia and the UAE on Meta and TikTok. The control: hook A, iftar in twenty minutes, a designed Arabic female voice, fifteen seconds, vertical. Variant one changes only the hook. Variant two changes only the voice. Variant three is a six-second cut. Localizations: Gulf Arabic as primary, English for expats, Urdu for South Asian audiences. Held constant: real product shots, legal-approved offer text, the call to action, caption style and the AI label policy.
```

Scene 7 — Example 1: a Manchester bakery

```text
First example, a simple one. A small bakery in Manchester makes two versions of a ten-second vertical ad for its weekend brunch. Same real footage of the pastries, same offer, same voice. Only the hook changes: fresh croissants every Saturday, versus skip the queue, preorder brunch. Each link carries a different UTM content tag. After two weekends, the preorder hook drives noticeably more orders on their site, illustratively. They didn't need a big budget. They needed one clean comparison.
```

Scene 8 — Example 2: Karachi skincare

```text
Second example, a business case with illustrative results. A Karachi skincare brand makes a master thirty-second explainer in Urdu with real product footage and a designed narrator. Then nine variants: three hooks, two voices, two lengths and an English version for the UAE diaspora. Every variant has its own UTM content value and a platform AI label where the voice is synthetic. After two weeks, one hook clearly wins on cost per purchase, while voice differences are small. They scale the winning hook, keep the cheaper voice, and record the learning in a creative log.
```

Scene 10 — Watch me do it, part 2

```text
Before production, I check two things. Every claim is true and approved: the twenty minutes, the delivery by Asr, the noon cutoff. And nothing generated could be read as the product or a result. Then I set up measurement. Each variant gets a UTM content value that describes it, like hook B, voice Arabic male, fifteen seconds. I choose one success metric that matches the objective, here cost per purchase, and I agree a minimum test duration and budget before launch, so nobody declares a winner after one lucky day.
```

Scene 11 — Honest measurement

```text
Now measurement, done honestly. Match the metric to the objective: hook rate and hold for attention, click-through for traffic, and cost per key event or cost per purchase for sales. Respect consent and privacy in how you measure. For consent-aware tracking, server-side tagging and conversion APIs, the Privacy-First Measurement course goes deep, and Web Analytics with GA4 shows how to report on variants. And give tests enough time and volume. A single day's result is mostly noise.
```

Scene 12 — AI and human assets together

```text
Here's a pattern I see again and again. A London software company launches a feature with a sixty-second AI-voiced explainer using real interface recordings, a twenty-second founder clip filmed on a phone, and dubbed Arabic and Spanish versions with native review. The founder clip wins with existing customers, who value the personal touch. The explainer wins with cold audiences, who need the what and the why. AI-produced and human-filmed assets aren't rivals. They're complementary tools for different audiences.
```

Scene 13 — Common mistakes

```text
Common mistakes. Testing five things at once, so you can't tell what won. Fake customer testimonials made with avatars or generated people. Generated shots that exaggerate product results. Declaring winners after a day, or without UTMs to separate variants. And forgetting that sponsorship disclosure and AI labels are separate. Each mistake either wastes the test or damages trust, and trust is the asset you're really building.
```

Scene 14 — Recap

```text
Recap. Treat marketing video as a testing system. Keep a control and change one variable per variant. Use AI for voices, hooks, localization and atmosphere, and keep products, prices, offers and people real. Tag every variant, measure the metric that matches your objective, and give tests time. And if you want to go beyond video into interactive voice, like an agent that answers product questions or books demos, the Voice AI Agents course is your next step.
```
