Scene 1 — Search intent

```text
Here's a question that saves more wasted content budget than any tool. Before you write a single word: what does the person typing this query actually want? Not what you want to sell them. What they want. In this lecture you'll learn the four types of search intent, how to read intent straight from the results page, how AI answers are changing which intents still earn clicks, and how to log it all in a simple sheet.
```

Scene 2 — Why intent matters

```text
Why does intent matter so much? Because search engines aim to show the result that best satisfies what the searcher wants. If your page doesn't match that need, no amount of keyword placement will keep it ranking. You can write a brilliant three thousand word guide and still lose to a simple product page, because the searcher wanted to buy, not to learn. Matching intent is the first rule of modern SEO.
```

Scene 3 — Four intent types

```text
There are four classic intent types. Informational: the searcher wants to learn, like how to start a podcast, or what is VAT in the UAE. Navigational: they want a specific site, like Daraz login. Commercial investigation: they're comparing before buying, like best CRM for small business. And transactional: they want to act now, like buy abaya online Dubai, or book dentist Lahore. Many queries blend intents. Wedding photographer Manchester is commercial and local at once.
```

Scene 4 — Read the SERP

```text
Here's the key idea. The fastest way to understand intent is to search the query yourself and study the page. Think of it like a restaurant critic checking what's already on the menu at the best restaurants in town before opening a new one. Look at four things. The content types ranking: blog posts, product pages, videos, tools, forums. The format: steps, lists, comparisons, calculators. The angle: for beginners, twenty twenty-six, cheap, near me. And the SERP features: AI Overviews, People also ask, local packs, videos, shopping.
```

Scene 5 — Match, then beat

```text
Here's what that tells you. If the top results are all product category pages, a long blog post will struggle. If they're all guides, a product page will struggle. Match the dominant type and format, then aim to be better. And map intent to your funnel. Informational pages build awareness. Commercial pages provide comparison and proof. Transactional pages must make buying or booking easy. Connect them with internal links that guide people from learning to buying.
```

Scene 6 — Intent + AI answers

```text
Now the AI part. AI Overviews and AI Mode change how intent is satisfied. Simple factual intents are increasingly answered right on the results page. What time is Maghrib in Dubai today rarely needs a click. And complex questions fan out. Google describes query fan-out, where its AI runs several related searches and combines the results. So a page that answers one sub question exceptionally well can be cited. Add a question to your analysis: what would make someone still click?
```

Scene 7 — Why people still click

```text
The answer is usually one of these. Depth. First hand experience. Tools like calculators. Current prices. Local specifics. Or the ability to act: book, buy, contact. ```
```

Scene 1 — Keyword research that finds real opportunities

```text
Most keyword research produces a spreadsheet of big numbers that nobody acts on. Good keyword research produces a prioritised map of topics your audience actually uses, matched to pages you can realistically rank and that support business goals. In this lecture you'll learn a six step process, how to use Search Console and Google Trends as hands on sources, and how to avoid the classic trap of creating five pages for one idea.
```

Scene 2 — Why it matters

```text
Why does this matter? Because volume is seductive and misleading. A high volume head term might be dominated by global brands you can't beat, or have an intent that doesn't match your business. Meanwhile, lots of specific, lower volume queries convert well and are winnable. Good research points your limited content budget at the topics that will actually pay back.
```

Scene 3 — Step 1: real customer language

```text
Step one: start with seeds from real customers. Customer questions in emails, WhatsApp chats and sales calls. Reviews, yours and competitors'. Forums and communities. Comments on YouTube and TikTok videos. Write down the words customers use, not your internal jargon. A Pakistani fintech might say digital wallet, while customers search how to send money from JazzCash to bank. That gap between your words and theirs is where opportunities hide.
```

Scene 4 — Step 2: expand with tools

```text
Step two: expand with tools. Google Search Console, for queries you already appear for. Keyword Planner, for volume ranges and ideas. Autocomplete, People also ask and related searches. Third party SEO tools like Semrush, Ahrefs or Moz for estimated volume, difficulty and competitor keywords. And YouTube and TikTok search suggestions for video opportunities. All volumes are estimates. Use them to compare, not as forecasts.
```

Scene 5 — Steps 3–4

```text
Step three: evaluate each keyword on five questions. Relevance: would this searcher be a potential customer? Intent: which content type ranks, and can we make it? Volume: is there meaningful demand for our niche? Difficulty: who ranks now, big brands or small sites and forums? And business value: how close is it to a sale or lead? Step four: favour the long tail, longer specific queries that are easier to win and convert well, especially for newer sites.
```

Scene 6 — Step 5: group by topic

```text
Here's the key idea behind step five. Group keywords by topic, not one page per keyword. Think of it like a clothes shop. You don't build a separate shop for blue jeans, denim trousers and jeans for men. It's one rail, well organised. Search engines understand synonyms. If the results pages for several keywords largely overlap, they belong on one page, with one primary keyword and secondary variants. That prevents keyword cannibalisation, where your own pages compete with each other.
```

Scene 7 — Step 6: prioritise

```text
Step six: prioritise. Score each topic from one to three on business value, ranking feasibility and effort, and start with high value, feasible, low effort topics. Now the hands on part. For an existing site, export your Search Console queries and add three formulas from the lesson. One flags striking distance queries, positions eight to twenty with decent impressions. One flags question queries starting with how, what, why and so on. And one separates brand from non brand.
```

Scene 8 — Seasonality with Google Trends

```text
Add seasonality. Search volumes in tools are averages, but demand in many markets swings with Ramadan, Eid, back to school, summer travel, Black Friday or White Friday, and tax deadlines. Check each priority topic in Google Trends for the right country over five years, note the peak months, and publish or refresh six to eight weeks before the peak, so the page is indexed and current when demand rises. Remember Trends shows relative interest, not absolute volume.
```

Scene 9 — Example 1 (simple)

```text
Worked example one, simple. A UK online plant shop exports Search Console queries. ```
```

```text
That Search Console control lets a site opt out of AI features in Search and Discover without affecting normal results. It's a genuine business decision. Weigh visibility against your content use concerns, check the current Search Console help, and decide deliberately rather than by accident. Other AI companies publish their own crawler names, like GPTBot, OAI SearchBot, ClaudeBot and PerplexityBot, each with its own documentation.
```

<details><summary>Narration per scene (paste into ElevenLabs)</summary>

Scene 1 — Search Console + Python + Sheets

```text
The Search Console interface is great, until you need more than a thousand rows, or want to see query and page together for three months, every month, without clicking for an hour. That's when a little Python pays for itself. In this lecture you'll set up the Search Console API, write a reusable extractor with pagination, run four practical analyses, and get the results into Google Sheets or Looker Studio.
```

Scene 2 — Why the API

```text
Why go beyond the interface? It shows up to a thousand rows per table and one comparison at a time. The API returns far more rows, combines dimensions like query, page and date, and makes monthly analyses repeatable. Data is available for roughly the last sixteen months, so export regularly for longer history, or use Search Console's bulk export to BigQuery on large sites.
```

Scene 3 — One-time setup

```text
Setup is a one time job. In Google Cloud, create a project and enable the Search Console API. Create a service account and download its key, stored outside your code folder, with an environment variable pointing to it. Then, in Search Console settings, add the service account's email as a user. Restricted access is enough to read data. Finally install the Google API client, Google auth and pandas.
```

Scene 4 — The extractor

```text
Here's the key idea behind the extractor. Think of it like a librarian fetching records in boxes. Each request can return at most twenty five thousand rows, so the script asks for the first box, then the next, using a start row parameter, until a box comes back less than full. It also retries politely with a short backoff if it hits a quota error. The result is one tidy table with your dimensions plus clicks, impressions, CTR and position.
```

Scene 5 — Four analyses

```text
Now four analyses. One: striking distance, non brand queries ranking eight to twenty with real demand. Two: low CTR for position. Here's a smart trick. Instead of generic industry CTR curves, which vary wildly by query type and SERP features, compare each row with your own site's median CTR at that position. Three: cannibalisation, queries where two or more of your pages each get meaningful impressions. And four: question queries, raw material for FAQs and cluster pages.
```

Scene 6 — Own benchmarks

```text
Why your own benchmark matters. Imagine two queries at position three. One has an AI Overview, a video carousel and shopping ads above the results. The other has plain blue links. Their normal CTR will be very different. Generic curves would flag the first as underperforming when it's actually typical. Comparing against your own median at each position is still rough, but it's grounded in your real results pages.
```

Scene 7 — Example 1 (simple)

```text
Worked example one, simple. A solo blogger with a cooking site runs just the question analysis once. It surfaces fifty questions she ranks for between positions five and fifteen, like can you freeze biryani and why is my naan dough sticky. She adds short, answer first sections to the relevant recipes, and writes two new posts for the biggest gaps. One script, one afternoon, a month's worth of content ideas grounded in real demand.
```

Scene 8 — Example 2 (illustrative)

```text
Worked example two, realistic and illustrative. A Karachi edtech company's content lead runs the script on the first working day of each month. Striking distance surfaces forty non brand queries, and the team refreshes six pages covering them. The low CTR check finds a guide whose title promises free courses the site no longer offers. Fixing the title restores CTR. Cannibalisation shows two near identical posts on freelancing skills, which get consolidated. The routine takes about two hours and replaces guesswork with a short, prioritised list.
```

Scene 9 — Into Sheets / Looker Studio

```text
Getting results into Sheets is easy. Save each table as a CSV and import it. For no code users, Sheets add ons and the Looker Studio Search Console connector can pull data directly, and Looker Studio is a good way to share dashboards. In Sheets, a few formulas go a long way: a brand flag with REGEXMATCH, CTR versus your median with XLOOKUP, and a top pages summary with QUERY.
```

Scene 10 — Read cannibalisation carefully

```text
Let's pause and read the cannibalisation output properly, because it's easy to over react. Suppose the query UAE VAT registration shows three of your URLs. One is your registration guide, one is your VAT service page, and one is an old news post about a threshold change. Is that a problem? The guide and the service page serve different intents, informational and transactional, so both can be right. The old news post is the one to look at. If it's outdated and competes with the guide, merge its useful bits and redirect it.
```

Scene 11 — Work responsibly

```text
Work responsibly. Keep the service account key out of code and version control, and restrict who can read it. Remember that some rare queries are anonymised, so query level totals won't match page totals. And annotate site releases, updates and campaigns in a shared log, so trends can be interpreted. Common mistakes: pulling only a thousand rows and assuming it's everything, comparing query and page totals, using generic CTR curves, and hard coding credentials.
```

Scene 12 — Recap

```text
Recap. The Search Console API gives you more rows, combined dimensions and repeatable analyses. Use a service account with the key path in an environment variable, and paginate. Run striking distance, low CTR for position, cannibalisation and question analyses, benchmark against your own site, and share results through Sheets or Looker Studio. Try this now. Run the extractor for last quarter, or use an export, and produce a one page list of your top ten actions with the evidence for each.
```
