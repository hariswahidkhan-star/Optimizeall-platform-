Scene 1 — Ask, Check, Own

```text
Here's a question for you. Two people use exactly the same AI assistant for a month. One saves five hours a week. The other sends a client an email that promises a discount nobody approved. Same tool. So what's the difference? It isn't talent, and it isn't some secret prompt. It's a habit. Three words: Ask, Check, Own. In the next few minutes you'll learn that habit, you'll watch me apply it to a real email, and you'll leave with a checklist you can use today, in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot or anything else.
```

Scene 2 — Why this matters now

```text
Why does a habit matter so much? Because AI assistants have changed. A couple of years ago, they mostly produced text in a chat box. Today, many of them can also read your documents, search your inbox, fill in spreadsheets, and even carry out multi step tasks while you get on with something else. ChatGPT has an agent called ChatGPT Work. Claude can work across your files and apps. Microsoft 365 Copilot and Gemini have agents and scheduled actions. That's genuinely useful. But here's the key idea. The more an assistant can do, the more it matters that you stay in charge. A wrong sentence in a draft is annoying. A wrong action sent in your name is a real problem.
```

Scene 3 — The new colleague analogy

```text
So let's use an analogy. Imagine a very smart new colleague on their first day. They're fast, they write well, and they want to help. But they don't know your clients, your history or your rules. First, you'd brief them properly. That's Ask. Tell the AI who the work is for, what you want, and paste the facts it needs. Next, you'd review their first piece of work before it went to a client. That's Check. And finally, if the work goes out under your name, you're the one answering for it. That's Own. Nobody accepts "the new person wrote it" as an excuse, and nobody accepts "the AI wrote it" either.
```

Scene 4 — Check: scale it to the stakes

```text
Now, checking doesn't mean spending an hour on every output. Scale it to the stakes. Low stakes, like a brainstorm or a rough internal draft? Skim it for sense. Thirty seconds. Medium stakes, like a routine client email or a message to your manager? Read every line. Check names, dates, numbers, and especially promises. Two or three minutes. High stakes, like a contract, a price, a public post, or anything legal, financial, medical or HR? Verify every fact against the source, and get a qualified human to review it. That ladder keeps you fast on small things and careful on the things that can hurt you.
```

Scene 5 — Own: more than hitting send

```text
Own deserves a little more attention, because it's more than hitting send. Owning the output means three things. First, you followed your organization's AI policy, including which tools are approved for which kinds of information. Second, you're honest about AI help where that's expected. Some clients write it into contracts, schools and universities have their own rules, and many social platforms ask you to label realistic AI generated images and video. Third, if there's a sentence you can't verify, it doesn't go out. Cut it, or mark it as something to confirm. That last one sounds strict, but it's freeing. You never have to defend a claim you didn't check.
```

Scene 6 — Example 1: the parking notice

```text
Let's make this concrete with a simple example. Aisha is an office manager in Dubai. She needs to announce a new visitor parking process. She gives her work assistant her bullet notes and asks for a short, friendly announcement. That's Ask. The draft is good, but one sentence says visitors no longer need to register. That's wrong. They still register, just at reception instead of online. Her Check step catches it in seconds. She fixes the line, confirms the times, and sends it. That's Own. Five minutes instead of twenty, and no confused visitors on Monday morning. Notice what the AI did well, which is wording, and what Aisha did, which is facts.
```

Scene 7 — Example 2: the agency follow-up

```text
Here's a more realistic business scenario. The numbers are illustrative. Imagine a small marketing agency in Lahore with one account lead, Omar, who sends around thirty client follow up emails a week. He starts using Ask, Check, Own. For every email, he writes his decisions first as three bullets, then lets the assistant draft the wording. After a week, he estimates he's saved about two hours. But here's the interesting part. His Check step caught one problem almost every week. Once the draft promised delivery by Friday when the designer had said Monday. Once it offered a free extra revision. Each catch took ten seconds to fix. Without the habit, each one could have cost him a client's trust or a day of unpaid work.
```

Scene 8 — Watch me do it

```text
Let me show you how I do this. I've got a supplier email asking to raise their price and move a delivery date. First, before I touch the AI, I write my decisions. Accept the new date. Refuse the price increase. Ask for a call about quality. Next, I paste the email, minus the supplier's phone number, because the AI doesn't need it. I add my three decisions, the tone, friendly but firm, and a length, under one hundred and twenty words. The draft comes back in seconds. Now I check. Names, correct. Dates, correct. But look at this line. It says we'll consider the new pricing. I never said that. I refused it. So I change consider to we can't accept. That one word is the whole point of the Check step. Then I read it once more, and I send it under my name.
```

Scene 9 — Common mistakes

```text
Let's look at the mistakes I see most often. The first is copy, paste, send. It feels efficient until the day it isn't. The fix is simple: read before you send, every time. The second is pasting private information into a personal account. Client documents, colleague details, anything confidential belongs only in tools your organization has approved, and passwords, card numbers and ID numbers never go in at all. The third is subtle. It's letting the AI decide what you agree to. When you just say reply to this, the AI guesses your position, and it usually guesses the polite, accommodating one. Decide first. Let the AI handle the wording. And one bonus mistake: using AI to write about something you don't understand. If you can't check it, you can't own it.
```

Scene 2 — Why 'summarize this' disappoints

```text
Why does summarize this disappoint? Because a summary isn't a neutral thing. The same report needs a two line version for a CEO, an action list for the project team, and a risk list for finance. When you don't say who it's for, the AI picks the safest option, which is usually a restatement of the introduction. Tell the AI who needs the summary, what they'll do with it, and how you'll check it.
```

Scene 3 — The map analogy

```text
Think of summaries like maps. One city, many maps. A tourist wants landmarks. A delivery driver wants routes and one way streets. A city planner wants zones and pipes. For a manager who must decide, you want the decision, the options, the costs and risks, and what's missing. For a team, you want actions, owners and deadlines. For a newcomer, you want plain language and the key terms. Same document, different maps.
```



Scene 1 — Translation and localization

```text
Imagine sending a beautifully translated promotion to customers in Riyadh, with every word correct, and it still falls flat. The offer is tied to a holiday they don't celebrate. Scene 2 — Why it matters
```

```text
Why does this matter? Because AI translation has become genuinely good, especially for widely used languages. For understanding a message, drafting a reply or internal use, it's often excellent. But quality still varies by language and topic, and less widely used languages generally get weaker results. And even perfect words can carry the wrong meaning or the wrong cultural signal. When that happens in an internal note, it's a small embarrassment. When it happens in a public ad, a product label or a customer contract, it's public, and sometimes costly. So we use AI for speed and a clear process for safety.
```

Scene 3 — The tour guide analogy

```text
Here's an analogy. A dictionary gives you words. A local tour guide gives you meaning. The guide knows which café locals actually go to, which days shops close, and which jokes won't land. Translation is the dictionary. Localization is the guide. It adapts tone, examples, idioms, currency, dates, measurements, cultural references and even legal requirements. A promotion that works in London might need a very different approach in Riyadh, Karachi or New York, even when it's all in English. AI can do a good job of both, if you ask for both, and if you check.
```

Scene 4 — The three-step method

```text
Here's the method. Step one, translate with a glossary. Tell the AI the language variety and audience, like Gulf Arabic for customers in the UAE, give it a short glossary of terms that must be used exactly, like your brand name and approved product terms, and ask it to flag any phrase that doesn't translate naturally. Step two, back translate. In a new chat, so it can't just copy your original, paste the translation and ask for a literal English version. Compare meaning, not wording. Step three, localize. Ask it to adapt the message for the market, and to list every change it made and why, as a table. That table teaches you about the market and makes review fast.
```

